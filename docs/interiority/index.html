How Language Directs Attention to Bodily Interiority

On the three-dimensionality of inner space, the words that open it, and the traditions that have learned to wield them.

Compiled from phenomenology, neuroscience, cognitive linguistics, and somatic education

Chapter One

Introduction

The research question, and why it matters

This booklet explores a deceptively simple question: How does language bring attention to the interior of the body? More precisely, how do verbal cues create the experience of exploring the three-dimensionality of inner space — the felt volume of the torso, the depth behind the sternum, the space between the sit bones?

The question sits at the intersection of several fields that rarely speak to one another. Phenomenologists have theorized what it means to inhabit a body from the inside. Neuroscientists have mapped the cortical circuits that fire when we hear action words. Cognitive linguists have traced how spatial metaphors — "inside," "through," "behind" — are grounded in bodily experience. And practitioners of somatic education — Feldenkrais, Alexander Technique, yoga, tai chi, dance — have developed sophisticated verbal cueing traditions without, for the most part, theorizing them.

The gap in the literature is striking. There is surprisingly little systematic writing about verbal guidance strategies from within any somatic education community. The knowledge lives in oral and training traditions, not in published scholarship. A yoga teacher knows that "let your sit bones widen" produces a different experience than "squeeze your glutes," but the mechanism behind this difference — and the principles for constructing effective cues — remain largely unarticulated.

Several threads converge to make this question tractable:

  • Motor imagery research shows that imagined movement activates similar neural pathways to actual movement — and that the verbal framing of imagery instructions modulates the depth and specificity of that activation.
  • Gendlin’s felt sense offers a philosophical account of how the right word physically shifts something in the body — not by describing a pre-existing state, but by carrying forward an implicit bodily process into new form.
  • Embodied cognition (Lakoff, Johnson) demonstrates that spatial concepts like "inside" and "through" are not abstract — they are grounded in the body’s own experience of containment, and they actively shape how we experience that interior.
  • Phenomenology (Leder, Merleau-Ponty, Sheets-Johnstone) reveals a paradox: the healthy body systematically hides its interior from awareness, and language-based somatic practices may constitute a distinctive way of reversing that concealment.

The practical question running beneath all of this: What makes a verbal cue somatically productive versus inert? “Lift your arm” is an instruction. “Sense the weight of your arm as it begins to float” is something else entirely. What is the difference, and what are the principles?

A Spectrum of Verbal Cues

Somatic instructions range from literal/anatomical (“flex your toes”) to metaphorical/evocative (“let your foot melt into the floor”) to paradoxical (“sense the space behind your eye”). The hypothesis explored across these chapters: the evocative and paradoxical end of the spectrum may produce richer interiority — more three-dimensionality, more sense of inner space — than the literal end.

Chapter Two

The Neuroscience of Language and Movement

Motor imagery, verbal cueing, and the somatotopic bridge

Hearing Action Words Activates the Body Map

The most striking finding in this domain is that simply hearing or reading words related to body actions activates the corresponding body-part regions in motor cortex, in a somatotopic (body-mapped) pattern. This happens rapidly, automatically, and without any intention to imagine movement.

Pulvermüller, Hauk, Nikulin & Ilmoniemi (2005) demonstrated via fMRI that reading words like “lick,” “pick,” and “kick” differentially activated regions along the motor strip that overlapped with areas activated by actual tongue, finger, and foot movements. The motor cortex literally fires in body-part-specific patterns during passive reading.

Somatotopic Representation of Action Words in Human Motor and Premotor Cortex, Neuron, 2005

Hauk, Shtyrov & Pulvermüller (2008) showed this activation occurs within approximately 250ms for visual words, and as fast as 140–170ms for spoken words — well within the window of automatic processing. Using a passive oddball paradigm where subjects were not attending to the words, they demonstrated activation occurred without direct attention to the stimuli.

The time course of action and action-word comprehension, J. Cognitive Neuroscience, 2008

Tettamanti, Buccino et al. (2005) found that simply listening to sentences describing mouth, hand, and leg actions activated effector-congruent sectors of premotor cortex — the same regions active during observation and execution of those actions.

Listening to action-related sentences activates fronto-parietal motor circuits, J. Cognitive Neuroscience, 2005

Key Implication

Language is not just about movement — it partially is movement, at the neural level. A verbally guided somatic lesson does not merely describe actions; it activates the motor circuits for those actions.

Verbal Specificity Shapes Motor Simulation

A critical nuance: the richness of the verbal instruction determines the precision of the motor response. A bare verb (“bend”) produces diffuse motor priming. A richly contextualized instruction (“slowly let the weight of your head draw your chin toward your chest, feeling the vertebrae open one by one”) produces far more specific and targeted motor simulation.

An fMRI study found that reading action verbs in minimal context produced a general, non-specific increase in motor excitability, whereas rich sentential context produced action-specific motor patterns.

Sentential Context Modulates Motor Cortex Involvement, Brain and Language, 2013

This means the craft of verbal instruction directly determines the precision of the somatic experience evoked. The quality of language is not decorative — it shapes which motor circuits fire and how specifically they engage.

Kinesthetic vs. Visual Imagery

The distinction between kinesthetic motor imagery (feeling yourself move) and visual motor imagery (seeing yourself move) maps onto fundamentally different neural architectures. Verbal cueing can steer the brain toward one or the other.

Guillot, Collet et al. (2009) used fMRI to show that kinesthetic imagery preferentially activated motor-associated structures (inferior parietal lobule, ventral premotor cortex, putamen, cerebellum), while visual imagery activated occipital and superior parietal regions. Kinesthetic imagery produced stronger engagement of motor planning and execution areas.

Brain activity during visual versus kinesthetic imagery, Human Brain Mapping, 2009

Subjects often have difficulty separating visual from kinesthetic imagery without specific instructions. Instructions emphasizing felt sensation (“notice the weight shifting through your hip joint”) drive kinesthetic imagery; instructions emphasizing spatial position (“imagine your arm rising to shoulder height”) drive visual imagery.

Design Constraint

For somatic exploration, kinesthetic imagery is the target modality. Sensation-oriented verbs and references to weight, pressure, temperature, and effort are functionally different from position-and-shape language. Verbal cueing must be crafted to evoke sensation rather than visualization.

Language Makes Muscles Twitch

Perhaps the most direct evidence that language drives embodied response comes from EMG studies. Research using surface electromyography showed that reading “I am smiling” activates the zygomatic muscle, while reading “I am not smiling” inhibits it — demonstrating that comprehension of action negation involves an initial motor simulation followed by inhibitory suppression.

Comprehension of action negation involves inhibitory simulation, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2013

Language does not stop at the brain — it propagates into the body as subthreshold muscular activation. A well-crafted verbal instruction creates measurable neuromuscular events even when the listener is lying still.

Verbal Framing as Gain Control

Wright, McCormick, Williams & Holmes (2016) demonstrated that the verbal instructions given to observers watching movement significantly modulated their corticospinal excitability. When told to simultaneously imagine performing the observed movement, motor evoked potentials were substantially greater than during passive observation. The same sensory input produces dramatically different levels of motor engagement depending on the accompanying verbal instruction.

Viewing Instructions Accompanying Action Observation Modulate Corticospinal Excitability, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2016

The Verbal-Motor Interface is Bidirectional

Kawasaki, Kono & Tozawa (2019) found that verbally describing one’s own body movement during a motor skill task produced significantly better acquisition than controls — fewer errors, faster performance, with benefits persisting the following day. Individual motor imagery ability correlated strongly with learning gains (r = −0.66).

Efficacy of Verbally Describing One's Own Body Movement in Motor Skill Acquisition, Perceptual and Motor Skills, 2019

Not only does hearing language activate motor circuits, but producing language about one’s own movement enhances motor learning. The verbal-motor interface runs in both directions.

Theoretical Foundations

Two theoretical pillars support the use of language as a primary tool for somatic exploration:

Barsalou’s grounded cognition (1999) proposes that all concepts, including those evoked by language, are represented as modal simulations — partial reactivations of the sensory-motor states that produced them. Understanding a word means running a simulation in the same neural systems that process the corresponding perception or action. Language is not decoded into abstract symbols; it is re-enacted.

Jeannerod’s motor simulation theory (2001) proposes that motor imagery and motor execution share the same neural representation, with imagery being the “covert counterpart” of any goal-directed action.

Can Language Alone Create Somatic Exploration?

The evidence converges on a strong yes, with qualifications. Language automatically activates motor circuits in a body-part-specific pattern. The depth of activation scales with verbal specificity. But the gap between automatic premotor priming (fast and shallow) and deliberate motor imagery (deep but effortful) means that effective verbal cueing must use language not just to name movements but to evoke them richly enough that automatic priming tips into full kinesthetic simulation.

Chapter Three

The Felt Sense

Gendlin on language, bodily knowing, and carrying forward

Core Concepts

The felt sense is Eugene Gendlin’s term for a bodily awareness that is pre-conceptual and pre-emotional — not a feeling in the ordinary sense, but the body’s holistic, implicit knowing of a situation. It is murky, unclear at first, and more complex than any single emotion. Gendlin distinguished it sharply from emotions: an emotion is already differentiated (“I’m angry”), while a felt sense is the wider, vaguer “all of that about this situation” that hasn’t yet found form.

Gendlin, E.T. (1981). Focusing. Bantam Books.  |  Gendlin, E.T. (1962/1997). Experiencing and the Creation of Meaning. Northwestern University Press.

Carrying forward is Gendlin’s most philosophically radical claim: the right word or symbol doesn’t merely describe a pre-existing internal state — it changes it. The felt sense is not a static object that language labels. When the precise word arrives, the felt sense physically shifts: the body releases tension, breathing changes, something loosens. The word and the body co-create a new meaning that didn’t exist before either one alone.

This breaks the standard representational model of language. For Gendlin, meaning is an interaction between symbols and bodily process. The felt sense implies further steps but doesn’t contain them as finished content. Language “carries forward” the body’s implicit process into explicit form, and in doing so, transforms both the language and the body.

Gendlin, E.T. (2004). “The New Phenomenology of Carrying Forward.” Continental Philosophy Review, 37(1), 127–151.

The Focusing Technique

Gendlin operationalized these ideas in six steps: (1) clearing a space, (2) choosing an issue, (3) finding the felt sense of it, (4) finding a “handle” — a word, phrase, or image that fits, (5) resonating — checking the handle against the felt sense, and (6) receiving — staying with whatever comes.

The critical moment is step five: the body itself confirms or rejects the word. A wrong word produces no shift or a tightening; the right word produces a physical release. This is not metaphorical — it is observable in breathing changes, muscle release, and shifts in neural activation patterns.

Surprising Findings

Language as Somatic Event

Gendlin’s most counterintuitive claim is that finding the right word is not a cognitive achievement — it is a somatic event. The body “recognizes” the word before the mind does. This inverts the usual hierarchy: the body is the primary meaning-maker; language serves the body’s knowing, not the other way around.

The Felt Sense is Generative, Not Retrievable

Gendlin insisted the felt sense is not a stored memory or feeling being accessed. It is freshly formed in the moment of attending. Each time you check in with your body about a situation, you get a new felt sense that incorporates everything that has happened since, including the previous focusing. This makes it fundamentally different from a body scan or emotion labeling.

The Zigzag

Gendlin described productive thinking as a constant zigzag between precise logical/conceptual work and checking back with the felt sense. He argued that major breakthroughs happen this way — not through logic alone, but through a felt sense of “something more” that hasn’t yet been formulated.

Gendlin, E.T. (2009). “What First and Third Person Processes Really Are.” J. Consciousness Studies, 16(10–12), 332–362.

Empirical Support

Therapy outcome research. Marion Hendricks compiled over 90 studies showing that successful therapy outcomes across modalities correlate with clients’ ability to access a felt sense. The Experiencing Scale (Klein et al., 1969), developed from Gendlin’s theory, reliably predicts therapy outcome: clients who reference bodily-felt meaning improve more than those who deal only in emotions or narratives.

Affect labeling. Lieberman et al. (2007) showed via fMRI that putting feelings into words reduces amygdala activation and activates the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex — providing a neural mechanism for the “felt shift.” The word doesn’t just describe the feeling; it literally changes the neural processing of it.

Lieberman et al. (2007), Putting Feelings into Words, Psychological Science, 18(5), 421–428.

Interoception neuroscience. Craig (2002) and Critchley & Garfinkel (2017) identified the insular cortex as the neural substrate for whole-body feeling-awareness — mapping closely onto Gendlin’s felt sense. The anterior insula integrates visceral signals into a “global emotional moment,” and people with higher interoceptive accuracy show better emotional regulation and decision-making.

Connections to Somatic Education

Both Gendlin and Feldenkrais rejected the mind-body split and held that awareness itself is transformative. But their entry points differ:

  • Feldenkrais enters through movement — using novel movement patterns to interrupt habitual motor schemas, allowing the nervous system to discover new options. Language is secondary.
  • Gendlin enters through language and attention — using words to access the body’s implicit knowing. Movement is secondary.
  • The deep convergence: both hold that the organism “knows more than it can say” and that the practitioner’s job is to create conditions for implicit knowing to come forward.

A Feldenkrais practitioner cueing “notice what happens in your ribs as you do this” is performing something very close to Gendlin’s “finding a handle” — offering language that may (or may not) produce a felt shift.

Don Hanlon Johnson connected Gendlin explicitly to the somatic education tradition, arguing that Alexander Technique, Feldenkrais, and Rolfing all work by the same fundamental mechanism: bringing pre-verbal bodily knowing into awareness through some form of symbolic mediation — words, touch, or movement cues.

Johnson, D.H. (1995). Bone, Breath, and Gesture: Practices of Embodied Awareness. North Atlantic Books.

The Practical Takeaway

The quality of language matters enormously. Generic or externally-imposed language (“relax your shoulders”) may produce compliance but not a felt shift. Language that emerges from or resonates with the person’s own felt sense (“it’s like something is gripping there”) produces genuine somatic reorganization. Verbal cues should evoke felt-sense recognition rather than impose conceptual categories.

Chapter Four

The Absent Body

Phenomenology of bodily interiority

Drew Leder: Two Structures of Absence

Leder’s The Absent Body (1990) offers the most directly relevant philosophical treatment. His core argument: the healthy body systematically conceals itself from awareness through two structures:

The ecstatic body. The sensorimotor body effaces itself by projecting outward — I don’t experience my eyes, I experience the visual field they disclose. The body disappears in the direction of its engagement with the world.

The recessive body. Internal organs disappear by “recessive infolding” — visceral processes (digestion, circulation, liver function) proceed without conscious apprehension. Leder identifies “phenomenological null points” where life-sustaining organs are absolutely absent from experience. Life itself is allied to a certain concealment.

Dys-appearance. The body’s interior becomes present primarily through breakdown — pain, illness, dysfunction. The paradox: we are brought to a heightened awareness of the body, but it is a body in which we no longer feel at home.

Drew Leder, The Absent Body (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1990)

Leder acknowledges that heightened introspection can partially overcome visceral disappearance, noting that Western insensitivities can be overcome by “systematic development of powers” — citing yogic training. Yet even advanced practitioners work within an overall context of experiential disappearance. Unlike vision’s continuous field, visceral awareness is fragmentary, gapped, and causally opaque.

Kristin Zeiler: Eu-Appearance

Zeiler (2010) extends Leder’s framework by identifying eu-appearance — the body becoming present as “good, easy, or well.” While Leder focused on pathological presence, Zeiler shows the body can also become thematically present through pleasure, physical exercise, sexual experience, and wanted pregnancy. This is the crucial philosophical bridge: the body’s interior need not only surface through pain.

A Third Mode of Bodily Presence

Feldenkrais instructions may facilitate eu-appearance — making the body’s interior present in a way that is exploratory and pleasurable rather than distressed. ATM language systematically invites non-pathological attention to the body’s interior, creating what Leder would recognize as a cultivated reversal of the body’s natural recession.

Kristin Zeiler, “A phenomenological analysis of bodily self-awareness in the experience of pain and pleasure”, Medicine, Health Care and Philosophy 13(4), 2010

Merleau-Ponty: Body Schema and Lived Spatiality

Merleau-Ponty’s distinction between body schema and body image is foundational. Body image is the conscious representation of one’s body. Body schema is the pre-reflective, action-oriented sense of the body’s capacities — “not an ‘I think that’ but an ‘I can.’”

Critically, Merleau-Ponty defines spatial understanding as a “spatiality of situation” rather than position. Space is constituted by active bodily engagement, not abstract geometry. The body schema is also “a certain structure of the perceived world” — changes in muscular tonus alter the perceived space itself. The body’s interior spatiality is not a static container but a dynamic, action-shaped field.

My body is a spatial object, and yet it is through it that spatiality and spatial objects are possible.

Thomas Fuchs: Leib and Körper

Fuchs works with the German phenomenological distinction between Leib (lived body) and Körper (objective body). The lived body is the transparent medium of experience; we act through it without attending to it. Fuchs introduces corporealization to describe what happens when this transparency breaks down: the lived body becomes an insistent obstacle rather than a medium.

Thomas Fuchs, Ecology of the Brain (Oxford UP, 2018)

Sheets-Johnstone: Movement as Mother Tongue

Sheets-Johnstone argues movement is “the mother of all cognition” and the foundation of our sense of space and time. She identifies four primary qualities of movement: tensional, linear, amplitudinal, and projectional — phenomenological (not anatomical) qualities of how movement feels from inside.

Her critique is sharp: even Lakoff, Varela, and Merleau-Ponty still privilege language and cognition over movement. They domesticate the body into a metaphor machine rather than taking kinesthetic experience on its own terms. For the question of bodily interiority, this is significant: the body’s interior is known through its movement qualities, not as an anatomical image.

We do not come into the world embodied. We come into the world moving; we are precisely not stillborn.

If deep somatic knowledge is pre-linguistic, then language as a tool for somatic exploration works against the grain. The practical question becomes: how close can language get? What verbal cues most effectively reactivate kinesthetic knowing rather than replacing it with conceptual understanding?

Cross-Cultural Evidence

A striking finding from Ma-Kellams (2014): non-Western cultures exhibit heightened somatic awareness (greater attention to bodily sensations, richer visceral vocabulary) yet lower interoceptive accuracy in laboratory heartbeat-detection tasks.

  • Chinese traditional medicine positions cognition in organs: the liver for contemplation, gallbladder for judgment, heart as executive center.
  • Japanese maps anger progression through body regions: belly (hara), chest (mune), head (atama).
  • West African Ewe has the term seselelame — literally “feel-feel-inside-the-body.”

The paradox: rich cultural schemas for the body’s interior may actually substitute for direct interoceptive sensing, creating strong conceptual awareness that doesn’t correspond to improved perceptual accuracy. Language and cultural framing actively shape how people experience the body’s interior.

Ma-Kellams, C. “Cross-cultural differences in somatic awareness and interoceptive accuracy”, Frontiers in Psychology, 2014

Developmental Construction

Children develop internal body knowledge through three stages: (1) sensorimotor — unconscious bodily self-knowledge, (2) visuospatial — ability to point to and name body parts, (3) lexical-semantic — language-based knowledge of functions and locations. Interior body awareness is not innate as a spatial representation but is progressively constructed through experience and language.

The Phenomenological Synthesis

The body’s interior is normally absent from awareness (Leder). Absence can be reversed through pathological breakdown, positive embodied experience (Zeiler), or cultivated attention. Interior body space is not experienced as anatomical but as a kinesthetic, action-oriented field (Merleau-Ponty, Sheets-Johnstone). Language actively shapes interior experience (cross-cultural evidence). And interior awareness is developmentally constructed, not given — built through sensorimotor experience and progressively scaffolded by language.

Chapter Five

Schemas and Metaphors

Container schemas, image schemas, and embodied cognition

The Container Schema

Mark Johnson introduced the concept of “image schemas” in The Body in the Mind (1987) — recurring patterns of sensory-motor experience that structure understanding. The containment schema is among the most fundamental: it consists minimally of a boundary, an interior, and an exterior.

Johnson illustrates its pervasiveness by noting that even a single morning involves dozens of containment experiences — getting out of bed-covers, into clothing, out of rooms, into the bathroom. These are not metaphors layered onto neutral experience; they are the pre-conceptual structures through which experience becomes intelligible.

Mark Johnson, The Body in the Mind (Chicago, 1987). Lakoff & Johnson, Philosophy in the Flesh (Basic Books, 1999).

The Body as the Original Container

The container schema is not derived abstractly — it arises from the body itself. From early infancy, we experience our bodies as three-dimensional containers: things go in (food, breath) and come out (excretions, exhalations, voice). The skin constitutes a boundary; visceral sensations register an interior.

Johnson argues that this bodily containment experience is the source domain for all subsequent containment reasoning, including logical categories (“Are tomatoes in the fruit or vegetable category?”) and emotional states (“trapped in grief,” “filled with joy”). The entailments are structural: if A is in B and B is in C, then A is in C — a logical rule Johnson claims is underwritten by pre-conceptual bodily experience of nested containment.

The Felt Dimension

In The Meaning of the Body (2007), Johnson extends the framework to address what the earlier work left underdeveloped: the qualitative, felt dimension of bodily experience. He argues that meaning-making involves “felt qualities, images, feelings, and emotions that ground our more abstract structures of meaning.” He is specifically concerned that cognitive analyses “tend to overlook the crucial role of qualities, patterns of feeling and the emotional contours of life.”

This represents Johnson’s attempt to bridge the gap between the structural account of image schemas and the qualitative texture of lived somatic experience — how containment does not merely have a logic but a feel.

Mark Johnson, The Meaning of the Body (Chicago, 2007).

Gallagher: Body Schema as Spatial Ground

Shaun Gallagher’s How the Body Shapes the Mind (2005) distinguishes body image (conscious perception of one’s body) from body schema (the pre-reflective sensorimotor system that shapes perception and action). Proprioception enables a non-conscious “proprioceptive self” from prenatal development onward.

Gallagher argues that the “interior” of the container schema is not simply a conceptual space but is grounded in proprioceptive and interoceptive signals that constitute a pre-reflective sense of volumetric selfhood.

Shaun Gallagher, How the Body Shapes the Mind (Oxford, 2005).

Sheets-Johnstone’s Critique

Sheets-Johnstone offers the most direct challenge to the Lakoff/Johnson framework. In The Primacy of Movement (1999), she argues that Johnson’s primary concern remains language, and that his “image schemata” are still cognitive representations — however “embodied” they claim to be. The foundational aspects of animate life — kinesthesia, the tactile-kinesthetic body, the developing coordination dynamics of infancy — do not enter the picture.

For the question of bodily interiority, this critique is significant: the container schema treats the body’s interior as a static spatial region (inside a boundary), whereas kinesthetic experience of the interior is dynamic — breathing expands and contracts it, visceral movement reshapes it, postural shifts redistribute the felt volume. The interior is not a fixed space but a moving, pulsing, shifting field.

Is the Container Schema Innate?

A 2017 paper in Human Development asks directly whether the container is a natural cognitive primitive. Sonesson and Lenninger argue that children do not interact with objects as though a generic container structure pre-exists; rather, they use “container” objects to fulfill different pragmatic functions developmentally. The container schema may be a developmental, pragmatic, and cultural product rather than a natural given.

Sonesson & Lenninger, “Is Container a Natural and Embodied Image Schema?”, Human Development 60(4), 2017.

The Neuroscience of the Interior

Contemporary interoception research provides the empirical substrate for what the container schema gestures toward conceptually. The insula integrates signals from the viscera with emotional and motivational factors, producing a complex representation of the body’s physiological state. Body awareness encompasses both “interoceptive and somatosensory awareness” — we perceive the body not only from the outside but also from the inside.

This suggests the “interior” in the container schema is not merely a logical space but corresponds to actual neural processing of visceral, proprioceptive, and interoceptive signals — the body’s interior is sensorially populated, not abstractly defined.

Ferrara, C. et al., “An Overview of Bodily Awareness Representation and Interoception”, Brain Sciences 14(4), 2024.

The Gap Between Schema and Soma

The container schema captures something real — the body does have an inside and an outside, and this distinction organizes vast amounts of reasoning. But there is a persistent tension between the schema’s topological simplicity (boundary + interior + exterior) and the phenomenological richness of actually inhabiting a three-dimensional body. The schema describes the logic of containment; it does not describe the experience of interiority. For somatic practices, this gap matters: the practitioner works not with a person’s concept of their interior but with their felt sense of volume, weight distribution, breath movement, and skeletal-visceral relationship — phenomena that the container schema names but does not reach.

Chapter Six

The Craft of Cueing

Verbal cueing across movement traditions

The Attentional Focus Framework

The most robust line of evidence comes from Gabriele Wulf’s research program spanning 15+ years. Her constrained action hypothesis proposes two mechanisms:

  • External focus (attention on the movement’s effect on the environment) promotes automatic, self-organizing motor control. Example: “push the floor away” rather than “extend your legs.”
  • Internal focus (attention on body parts) induces conscious interference with automatic processes, degrading both performance and learning.

Meta-analyses confirm external focus superiority across tasks, age groups, and skill levels. However, a nuanced 2021 ACSM review notes internal cues may be appropriate for novices learning technique and for hypertrophy training where deliberate muscle engagement is the goal. Expert performers often use holistic focus rather than pure internal or external cues.

Wulf (2013), Attentional focus and motor learning: a review of 15 years

Analogy and Metaphor Instruction

An alternative to explicit internal cues is analogy learning — a single metaphorical instruction that implicitly encodes multiple movement parameters. The canonical example: teaching a topspin forehand by saying “move your hand as if it is traveling up the side of a mountain” rather than giving explicit rules about wrist angle, racket face, and follow-through.

Analogy learners report fewer explicit rules and show more resilient performance under pressure. Analogy cues bypass working memory, producing movement that is more effortless and efficient. This is directly relevant to somatic traditions where a single image (“let your spine pour like water”) can organize complex multi-joint coordination without analytical decomposition.

Masters & Poolton, implicit motor learning; Poolton et al. (2006)

Yoga: Does Global Framing Matter?

Csala et al. (2020) randomized 84 novice yoga students to three groups — “Sport” (biomechanical framing), “Spiritual” (philosophical/chakra/energy language), and control — with identical physical content across 10 sessions. The result: no interaction effect between framing condition and any outcome (spirituality, body awareness, mindfulness, affect).

The somatically productive work appears to happen at the level of specific moment-to-moment attentional cues, not at the level of overarching philosophy.

Csala et al. (2020), “Verbal Cuing Is Not the Path to Enlightenment,” Frontiers in Psychology

Alexander Technique: Directions as Mental Acts

The Alexander Technique provides the most explicit theory of how verbal formulation itself matters. The core directions: “Allow my neck to be free,” “Allow my head to go forward and up,” “Allow my back to lengthen and widen.”

Critical linguistic features:

  • “Allow/let” rather than “make/do” — signaling that the student should stop interfering with a natural process, not produce a new muscular effort.
  • Directional rather than positional — “forward and up” specifies a vector, not a destination. As Alexander formulated it: “There is no such thing as a right position, but there is such a thing as a right direction.”
  • Thinking, not doing — directions are given as thoughts, not muscular commands. The verbal cue is designed to prevent conscious muscular control from disrupting coordination.

Tai Chi: Intention Precedes Movement

Classical tai chi instruction operates on the principle “First the mind, later the body” and “First the intent moves, later the power follows.” The practitioner mentally “sends” attention along a pathway before the body follows.

The tension with motor learning research is notable: tai chi instruction is deeply internally focused yet produces high-quality movement in skilled practitioners. This may be because tai chi’s internal focus is process-oriented and sensory (“notice the weight settling into the ball of the foot”) rather than mechanical and analytical (“activate the tibialis anterior”) — aligning with the “holistic focus” category that recent research treats as a third option.

Dance: Imagery as Primary Verbal Tool

Eric Franklin’s imagery taxonomy (from Dynamic Alignment Through Imagery) distinguishes three types:

  • Direct imagery: mentally rehearsing the movement itself
  • Anatomical imagery: visualizing internal structures (“roll one vertebra at a time”)
  • Metaphorical imagery: natural-world analogies (“let your arms flutter like hummingbirds,” “imagine a geyser flowing from the bottom to the top of the pelvis”)

Franklin demonstrates how contrasting images produce different postural organizations: “imagining the head resting on the body” vs. “imagining the body hanging from the head” create subtly different alignment patterns from the same anatomical reality.

Laban: Effort Qualities as Vocabulary

Laban provides a systematic vocabulary for communicating movement quality through four effort factors:

FactorContinuumVerbal Descriptors
WeightLight — Strong“float” vs. “punch”
TimeSustained — Sudden“glide” vs. “flick”
SpaceIndirect — Direct“wring” vs. “dab”
FlowFree — Boundreleased vs. controlled

Laban’s insight: quality words organize movement differently than positional or anatomical words. Saying “float” produces a different neuromuscular organization than saying “slowly raise your arm with minimal force” — the single word encodes weight, time, and flow simultaneously.

Feldenkrais: Awareness-Directed Language

The Feldenkrais Awareness Through Movement lesson uses a distinctive verbal strategy: exploratory rather than prescriptive (“Notice what happens when...”), sensing-oriented (“Can you feel the weight shifting?”), explicitly non-corrective, and structured around difference-detection (“do the movement again and notice if anything has changed”).

By directing attention to sensation rather than technique, the method avoids loading working memory with explicit rules — aligning with the motor learning concept of implicit learning.

Chapter Seven

Synthesis

Cross-cutting themes and the principles of somatically productive language

The Convergence

Across neuroscience, phenomenology, cognitive linguistics, and six movement traditions, a coherent picture emerges of how language interacts with the body’s interior. Several themes cut across every chapter:

1. Language is not just about movement — it partially is movement. The neuroscience is unequivocal: hearing action words activates motor cortex somatotopically, propagates into measurable muscular activation, and can be modulated by verbal framing. Gendlin arrives at the same conclusion from the opposite direction: finding the right word is a somatic event, not a cognitive one. Language and bodily process are not separate systems connected by a bridge; they are aspects of a single, continuous process.

2. The body’s interior is normally hidden. Leder’s two structures of absence — the ecstatic body projecting outward, the recessive body folding inward — explain why interior attention requires active cultivation. The body disappears in the direction of its successful functioning. Somatic education is, philosophically, a practice of reversing this disappearance without the distress of pathological dys-appearance.

3. The quality of language determines the quality of somatic experience. Bare verbs produce diffuse motor priming; rich sentential context produces specific motor patterns. Generic instructions (“relax your shoulders”) may produce compliance without a felt shift. Metaphorical cues bypass working memory and engage implicit motor processes. The craft of language is not decorative; it is functionally constitutive of the experience it evokes.

4. Schemas name the logic of interiority but miss its texture. The container schema captures the topological structure of bodily experience (boundary, interior, exterior) but not its felt quality. Johnson’s later work and Gendlin’s felt sense tradition point toward the qualitative dimension. Sheets-Johnstone argues more radically that the body’s spatial experience cannot be captured by any schema, because schemas are still cognitive representations imposed on a dynamic, kinesthetic, pre-representational reality.

The Spectrum of Verbal Cues

Literal / Anatomical
“Flex your toes”
Metaphorical / Evocative
“Let your foot melt into the floor”
Paradoxical / Impossible
“Sense the space behind your eye”

Each position on this spectrum engages different cognitive and motor systems. Literal cues activate specific motor programs but may trigger conscious interference (Wulf’s constrained action hypothesis). Metaphorical cues bypass analytical processing and engage holistic motor simulation (Masters & Poolton). Paradoxical cues — instructions that cannot be followed as stated — force a novel attentional orientation that may be uniquely productive for interiority, because the attempt to sense something impossible recruits exploratory, non-habitual patterns of internal attention.

Six Principles of Somatically Productive Language

Cross-tradition patterns suggest the following principles:

1. Permissive Language Outperforms Command Language

Alexander’s “allow/let,” Feldenkrais’s “notice/sense,” and dance imagery’s “imagine” all avoid triggering the effortful, conscious motor control that degrades movement quality. A cue is more productive when it invites a process than when it demands an outcome.

2. Holistic Cues Outperform Analytical Decomposition

Analogy learning, Laban effort words, and Franklin imagery all show that a single evocative term or image can organize multi-joint coordination more effectively than a list of body-part instructions. Metaphors bypass working memory and engage implicit motor processes.

3. Process Orientation Outperforms Outcome Orientation

Alexander’s “means-whereby” vs. “end-gaining,” Feldenkrais’s emphasis on awareness over achievement, and tai chi’s “intention before action” all prioritize the quality of the ongoing process over hitting a target position.

4. Sensation-Directing Cues Outperform Position-Prescribing Cues

“Notice where your weight falls” (Feldenkrais) and “feel the ground under the ball of your foot” (tai chi) direct attention to proprioceptive information, enabling self-correction. “Put your foot here” prescribes a position without developing the sensory capacity to find it independently. Kinesthetic imagery engages motor circuits more deeply than visual imagery.

5. Quality Words Operate Differently Than Quantity Words

Laban’s insight: “float” organizes the body differently than “move slowly and lightly.” Certain words function as direct motor affordances rather than descriptions to be analytically decoded. The most productive cues evoke a quality rather than specify a parameter.

6. Global Narrative Framing May Not Matter Much

The Csala et al. yoga study suggests that whether you frame an identical practice as “sport” or “spiritual” does not differentially affect outcomes. The somatically productive work happens at the level of specific, moment-to-moment attentional cues, not at the level of overarching philosophy.

The Central Insight

Gendlin’s most radical contribution resolves what initially seems like a false dichotomy. Language neither “creates” somatic experience (constructivist view) nor merely “accesses” a pre-existing one (realist view). Instead, language and bodily process interact — the body implies more than it contains, and the right word carries that implicit process forward into a new form that is both linguistic and somatic simultaneously. The felt shift is the evidence: a physical change in the body produced by a symbolic event.

The best somatic instructions do not describe the body’s interior. They do not label it. They carry it forward — offering language precise enough to produce a felt shift, open enough to allow the body’s own knowing to lead, and evocative enough to reactivate kinesthetic experience rather than replace it with conceptual understanding.

The best Feldenkrais instructions arguably do exactly this: “Let the weight of your head tell you something about the shape of your spine” is not anatomy. It is an invitation back into kinesthetic thinking — language that gets as close as language can get to the pre-linguistic knowing that Sheets-Johnstone insists is our mother tongue.
