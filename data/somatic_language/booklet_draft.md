# The Language of Moving: A Field Guide to Somatic Teaching

*How the words you choose shape what your students feel, discover, and learn*

---

## Opening: Language Is Your Instrument

Here is a simple movement. A person lies on their back, and the teacher asks them to turn their head to the right.

Watch what happens when the language changes.

**Version A**: *"Turn your head to the right. Notice how far you go. Come back to the middle."*

**Version B**: *"Before you move, sense the weight of your head on the floor. Begin to let your head roll to the right — not turning it, but allowing gravity to take it. Where does this movement start? What happens in your ribs? Come back to the middle whenever you like."*

The movement is the same. The experience is completely different.

In Version A, the student executes a task. Their motor cortex fires, the head turns, they measure the range, they return. In Version B, something richer happens: the student first wakes up the sensory map of their head against the floor. The word "begin" draws their attention to the threshold between stillness and movement. "Allowing gravity" shifts them from effortful doing to receptive sensing. "Where does this movement start?" splits their attention between head and torso, creating awareness of connections they didn't know existed. And "whenever you like" hands the timing back to them.

Same five seconds of movement. Completely different nervous system experience.

This is the core premise of somatic teaching: **language is not description — it is action**. The words a teacher chooses don't merely convey information about a movement. They shape the quality, speed, attention, and felt sense of the movement itself. A skilled somatic educator treats language as their primary instrument — as precise and deliberate as a surgeon's hands, as expressive as a musician's phrasing.

This booklet comes from a study of 19 Feldenkrais lessons taught by seven different teachers, spanning Moshe Feldenkrais himself in the 1970s to contemporary practitioners working today. We analyzed the language — not what was being taught, but *how* it was being said — and found 12 distinct mechanisms through which language shapes embodied experience, along with recurring structural patterns, common pitfalls, and a surprising finding about stylistic diversity.

What follows is a practical guide to these tools. Each section explains a principle, illustrates it with real quotes from skilled teachers, and gives you something to listen for or try. By the end, you should be able to listen to any somatic lesson and hear what's happening beneath the surface of the words — and begin to make more deliberate choices in your own teaching.

Three things to keep in mind as you read:

**There is no single "right" way.** The teachers in this study use the same tools very differently, and they're all effective. Moshe Feldenkrais commanded; Jeff Haller invites; Scott Clark questions; Andrew Gibbons explains. The mechanisms are tools. Your teaching philosophy determines how you wield them.

**Every example is real.** All quotes come from actual lessons. Nothing has been invented to make a point.

**This is about your ear, not your vocabulary.** The goal isn't to memorize terminology. It's to develop a perceptual sensitivity — to hear the linguistic choices in real-time and understand what they're doing.

## Part I: The Tools

Language in somatic teaching works through a set of identifiable mechanisms — specific things a teacher does with words that produce specific effects in the student's experience. We found twelve. For learning purposes, they cluster into six groups based on what dimension of the student's experience they primarily act on.

---

### Cluster 1: What You Name

#### Naming Creates Presence + Sensory Channel Selection

When you name a body part, you don't just direct attention — you change the nervous system's representation of that part. It becomes more present, more available, more *real* in the student's body map. This effect is strongest for parts that are usually below awareness: sit bones, inner ribs, the tongue root, the space behind the eyes.

But *how* you name matters as much as *what* you name. The sensory channel you invoke — weight, pressure, muscular effort, spatial relationship, quantity — determines which neural pathways participate.

**What this sounds like in practice:**

The simplest form is anatomical naming. Andrew Gibbons, known for his precise anatomical language, directs students to:

> *"The anterior superior iliac spine"*
> — Andrew Gibbons

This is not jargon for its own sake. By naming a specific bony landmark, Gibbons asks the student to locate something precise — to wake up a point on the body map that most people have never attended to. The specificity *is* the instruction.

Compare this with how a Feldenkrais Manhattan training teacher approaches naming through spatial language:

> *"The exact place where the head touches your mat"*
> — FM training teacher

No anatomy here. Instead, the teacher names a contact point — a place defined by the relationship between body and surface. This activates spatial processing rather than anatomical knowledge.

Feldenkrais himself often named through developmental significance:

> *"The first contact with the outside world is the mouth"*
> *"The mouth is the most conscious part of ourselves"*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

Here the naming does double duty: it brings the mouth to awareness *and* frames it within a story about human development. The student attends to their mouth differently when they understand it as the first organ of contact with the world.

Perhaps the most striking form is naming absence — naming the difficulty of finding a body part at all. Scott Clark:

> *"For years I would have had a hard time swearing in a court of law that I had a neck."*
> — Scott Clark

This is radical. Instead of directing attention to the neck, Clark names the experience of *not being able to find it*. This validates the student who also can't feel their neck — and makes the search itself the lesson.

The sensory channel you choose when naming creates different kinds of presence:

**Proprioceptive** (weight, pressure, contact): *"Feel the weight of your head on the floor"* — body-position processing, relationship to gravity.

**Muscular identification**: Gibbons asks, *"Which side of the back of the waist do you feel contracts?"* — this directs attention past general sensation to specific muscle groups. It's a more demanding form of sensing.

**Spatial** (distance, direction, relationship): *"The distance between your shoulder and your ear"* — relational awareness, attending to the space between parts rather than the parts themselves.

**Effort/force**: *"How much effort are you using?"* / *"Can you do it with less push?"* — modulates the motor cortex directly. Asking about effort changes effort.

**Force-vector**: *"Where does the push go through your body?"* — the student must trace an imagined line of force through their skeleton. This is a spatial-kinesthetic hybrid that builds awareness of mechanical transmission.

**Quantitative**: A training teacher asks, *"How many inches are you lifting? Is it five? Is it ten?"* — numbers create a different quality of attention than qualitative sensing. The student must measure, not just feel.

**Listen for this**: Which sensory channels does the teacher use most? Some teachers stay almost entirely in one channel (proprioceptive is most common). Others shift channels within a single exploration — asking first about weight, then about distance, then about effort. The multi-channel approach creates a richer neurological map.

**Try this**: Take a simple instruction you normally give — say, "Feel your left sit bone." Now rewrite it in three different channels: proprioceptive ("Feel the weight on your left sit bone"), spatial ("Notice the distance between your two sit bones"), quantitative ("If your weight is distributed ten to the right, how much is on the left — six? eight?"). Try each version and notice how they create different kinds of attention in the student.

### Cluster 2: How You Frame the Action

#### Verb Frame + Temporal Framing

The grammatical frame of your movement verb — how you phrase the doing — is one of the most powerful variables in somatic teaching. It determines whether the student executes, explores, or observes.

**Imperative / direct**: *"Lift your arm."*
This activates voluntary motor planning. Clear, efficient — and may recruit habitual patterns. Feldenkrais used imperative framing predominantly. There's nothing wrong with it; it just does a specific thing.

**Invitation**: *"Allow your arm to float upward."*
The word "allow" shifts the student from doing to permitting. Movement *emerges* rather than being *executed*. Effort drops. Parasympathetic tone increases. Jeff Haller and Nick Strauss-Klein favor this frame.

**Conditional / inquiry**: *"What happens if your arm begins to rise?"*
Now the student is an observer of their own movement. They're not doing or allowing — they're watching with curiosity. This is the frame that most directly creates the "inner witness" that somatic education cultivates.

**Negation**: *"Without lifting your arm, imagine the intention to lift."*
This dissociates intention from action — motor planning fires without motor execution. It's the ideomotor effect harnessed as a teaching tool. Jeff Haller uses this with great precision:

> *"Imagine the intention to lift."*
> — Jeff Haller

Four words that ask the student to find the exact boundary between thinking about moving and actually moving.

**Discovery**: *"Find the support through your skeleton."*
The word "find" implies the organization already exists — it's waiting to be discovered, not created. This frame respects the student's intelligence and positions learning as uncovering rather than building.

**Literary / aesthetic**: Gibbons occasionally uses language that recruits the student's aesthetic sensibility as a movement organizer:

> *"Pour yourself to the side."*
> *"Seduce yourself into comfort."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

"Pour" organizes a fluid, continuous weight shift without a single biomechanical term. "Seduce" reframes the relationship to comfort as something you court rather than achieve. These are rare but extraordinarily effective — they bypass the motor-planning system entirely and organize movement through metaphor.

**Temporal framing** is equally powerful. Consider the difference between these:

- *"Turn your head"* (neutral — no temporal information)
- *"Begin to turn your head"* (focus on initiation — attention goes to the threshold between stillness and movement)
- *"Slowly, slowly let the turning continue"* (extends duration — attention stays in the middle of the movement)
- *"And come to rest"* (marks an endpoint — invites settling and integration)

The phrase "begin to" deserves special attention. It is one of the most powerful phrases in somatic teaching. It directs attention to the exact moment when stillness becomes movement — the threshold where the most learning happens. Most students rush past this transition. "Begin to" makes them stay.

Temporal framing can also become a constraint. Sabine Schmid-Blackeby makes timing the primary learning variable:

> *"Lift your head and your foot at precisely the same moment."*
> — Sabine Schmid-Blackeby

The simultaneity demand forces global organization. The student can't just move head then foot — they must coordinate their whole body to make both events happen at once. The timing constraint, not the movement itself, is the teacher.

And temporal patience — the deliberate extension of waiting — creates receptivity:

> *"Wait for the need to breathe to grow on its own."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This instruction asks the student to do nothing until the body speaks. It's the opposite of an imperative — and for many students, the hardest thing in any lesson.

**Listen for this**: Track the verb frames across a lesson. Does the teacher stay in one frame, or does the frame shift? In many well-crafted lessons, the language progresses from imperative (doing) → invitation (allowing) → conditional (observing) → imaginal (imagining). This arc mirrors the lesson's pedagogical trajectory: from action toward awareness.

**Try this**: Take one movement instruction and say it in four frames: imperative ("Roll your pelvis"), invitation ("Allow your pelvis to roll"), conditional ("What happens when your pelvis begins to roll?"), and negation ("Without rolling your pelvis, imagine the intention to roll it"). Notice how each frame changes what you attend to as you do (or don't do) the movement.

### Cluster 3: What You Ask

#### The Question as Somatic Tool

Questions addressed to the student's body — not their intellect — create a fundamentally different quality of attention than statements. A statement gives information. A question *creates* the experience needed to answer it.

> *"Is your left hip heavier or lighter than your right?"*

To answer, the student must attend to both hips simultaneously. The question *creates* the comparison — it didn't exist before the teacher asked.

> *"What happens in your breathing when you do this?"*

The student must split attention between the movement and the breath. The question *creates* the connection.

Questions that cannot be answered intellectually force sensory engagement. *"How far does this movement travel through your spine?"* cannot be answered by thinking — only by feeling. Questions with no correct answer — *"Which side feels longer?"* — normalize subjective experience as valid data.

But questions serve radically different functions across teachers:

**Comparison questions** create simultaneous dual attention:

> *"Is your left hip heavier or lighter than your right?"*

**Timing questions** create sequential attention:

> *"When does your head start moving? When do the ribs start?"*

**Identification questions** direct attention to specific recruitment patterns:

> *"Which side of the back of the waist do you feel contracts?"*
> — Andrew Gibbons

**Existential questions** address the ground of experience itself. Scott Clark asks:

> *"How do you know that hand is present?"*
> — Scott Clark

This is not a question about the hand. It's a question about knowing. It asks the student to attend not to sensation but to the *process of sensing* — to the fact that awareness itself is happening. This is the deepest level a somatic question can reach.

**Philosophical questions** reframe the relationship between self and body. Feldenkrais:

> *"Who is the heel? What sort of authority is that?"*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

This is philosophy embodied. By asking "who" the heel is, Feldenkrais gives it personhood — and asks the student to reconsider their relationship to a body part they've ignored for decades.

**Counterfactual questions** create comparison through imagination:

> *"Can you imagine what it would feel like if the instructions were given without anything about the pelvis?"*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

The student must imagine an alternative version of their experience — a negative space that highlights what the pelvis is actually contributing.

**Disturbance-detection questions** direct attention to minimal perturbation:

> *"Does that tiny movement disturb your breathing?"*
> — FM training teacher

The word "tiny" calibrates scale. The word "disturb" creates a listening quality — the student attends to whether something changes within a continuity. This is extremely refined attention.

**Listen for this**: What kind of questions does the teacher ask? Simple comparison questions are the bread and butter of most lessons. But existential and philosophical questions — the kind Clark and Feldenkrais use — create a fundamentally different quality of inquiry. They ask the student not just to sense, but to question the nature of sensing itself.

**Try this**: After your students do a movement, ask three questions in sequence: a comparison question ("Which side feels longer?"), a spatial question ("Where does the movement start?"), and an existential question ("How do you know?"). Notice how the quality of the room changes with each question. The first creates sensing. The second creates searching. The third creates wondering.

### Cluster 4: What You Withhold

#### Constraint-as-Teacher + Silence

If you tell a student "Differentiate your pelvis from your shoulders," they hear information. If you tell them "Keep your shoulders on the floor as you roll your pelvis," they *discover* differentiation — because the constraint makes it the only available path.

This is the most universal mechanism in somatic teaching, and the most varied. Across 19 lessons and seven teachers, constraints took far more forms than expected:

**Positional constraints** are the most familiar:

> *"Keep your shoulders on the floor as you roll your pelvis."*

The floor becomes the teacher. The student doesn't need to understand differentiation — they need to solve a spatial problem, and differentiation emerges as the solution.

**Attentional constraints** direct noticing without restricting movement:

> *"Notice what your ribs do when you turn your head."*

The student discovers a connection they weren't looking for. The teacher didn't explain the rib-head relationship — the question *created* the conditions for the student to find it.

**Temporal constraints** make timing the learning variable, as we saw with Schmid-Blackeby's simultaneous lifting. When arm and leg must move "at precisely the same moment," the student's entire organization must change to meet the demand.

**Decoupling constraints** separate things that normally go together:

> *"Hold your breath and continue the movement without breathing."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

Breath and movement are deeply linked. Decoupling them reveals how much the student depends on breath to organize effort — and forces them to find another way.

**Negative constraints** define the instruction by what NOT to do:

> *"Do NOT turn the pelvis."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

This is a constraint that works by prohibition. The student must actively inhibit a habitual coupling. They discover how much they *want* to turn the pelvis — which reveals the connection between pelvis and the movement more clearly than any explanation.

**Quantitative constraints** set boundaries through measurement:

> *"Lift your leg no more than a hover."*
> — OpenATM teacher

"No more than a hover" — perhaps one centimeter off the floor. This is a constraint on effort and range that forces the student into micro-movement, where sensing is richest.

**Comparative constraints** create before/after awareness:

> *"Same rotation, but now the pelvis moves equally with the chest."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

"Same rotation" holds the theme constant while changing the distribution of participation. The student discovers the difference between chest-led and whole-body rotation.

And perhaps most important — **constraint removal**. After working within a constraint, the teacher removes it: *"Now do the movement freely and notice what changed."* The contrast between constrained and unconstrained movement makes the learning visible. This is the moment students often gasp — when they discover that something shifted while they weren't trying to change anything.

**Silence** is the ultimate form of withholding. A pause after an instruction gives the nervous system time to process and respond. Silence after a movement invites integration — the student discovers what changed without being told. The ratio of instruction to silence is a fundamental teaching variable, though it's impossible to evaluate from transcripts alone.

Multiple teachers across the corpus name rest as essential, suggesting shared awareness of this variable. One training teacher said to students, *"It's a sign of intelligence to rest"* — reframing silence not as downtime but as the learning moment.

**Listen for this**: Count the types of constraints in a single lesson. A well-crafted lesson might use positional, attentional, temporal, and negative constraints — each revealing a different aspect of the same movement theme. Also notice when the teacher is *silent*. The pauses between instructions are where integration happens.

**Try this**: Design a three-minute exploration using three constraint types. Start with a positional constraint ("Keep your knees together as you roll to the side"), add an attentional constraint ("Notice which part of your back lifts first"), then add a temporal constraint ("Make the rolling take the entire length of your exhale"). Each constraint reveals something different about the same movement.

### Cluster 5: What You Invoke

#### Image and Metaphor + Imagination as Movement + Agency Attribution

Some language bypasses analysis entirely. It speaks to the body's pattern-recognition system — the part that knows how to organize movement before the thinking mind gets involved.

**Images and metaphors organize movement without explanation.**

> *"Let your spine be like a chain."*

This single image organizes sequential, segment-by-segment movement without a word about vertebral mechanics. The student's body knows what a chain does. It knows that each link moves because the one before it moved. The image *is* the motor program.

> *"Pour yourself to the side."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

"Pour" creates fluid, continuous weight shift. There's no biomechanical vocabulary, no instruction about which body part leads — the metaphor handles all of it. The student's nervous system knows how liquid pours, and it organizes the body accordingly.

But images serve different functions:

**Movement-organizing images** directly shape motor patterns: *"Your spine like a chain"* or a training teacher's *"the shelf your leg lies on"* — these images tell the body how to organize.

**State-organizing images** shape the quality of the experience. Nick Strauss-Klein uses warm, permissive imagery that creates safety:

> *"Imagine the floor pushing you up."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This inverts the student's relationship to gravity. Instead of pushing against the floor, they *receive* from it. The muscular reorganization that follows — less effort, more support — emerges from the image, not from instruction.

**Narrative images** create a conceptual framework for physical experience. Feldenkrais told stories during lessons — about Darwin observing children, about infant development, about the evolution of upright posture. These narratives aren't tangents. They give the movement a context, a *meaning*, that changes how the student engages.

**Structural images** organize an entire lesson. In one training lesson, the teacher uses a clock face as a spatial reference — directing the student to push their foot toward twelve o'clock, then three, then six. The clock becomes the architecture of the whole exploration, not just a momentary image.

An important finding: **image density is a style variable, not a quality marker.** Clark and Feldenkrais use almost no imagery, relying on direct instruction and questions. Haller and Strauss-Klein use images frequently. Both approaches are effective. The choice reflects the teacher's theory of how learning happens, not a universal best practice.

**Imagination as movement** takes imagery further — into territory where imagined movement substitutes for physical movement entirely. This has deep roots in motor learning research, and Feldenkrais practitioners have used it from the beginning.

Strauss-Klein demonstrates the power of this approach: in one lesson on the carriage of the head, he teaches the entire left side through imagination alone. The student doesn't physically move their left side — they imagine the movements they've done on the right. When they finally do the movement physically, they find the left side has changed.

A training teacher makes the preparation step explicit:

> *"Organize it in your imagination. Get the order sensed and then go ahead."*
> — FM training teacher

"Get the order sensed" — the student must build the movement's sequence in their mind before executing it. This creates motor planning without execution, and it's especially powerful for non-habitual movements where existing motor programs would interfere.

Haller uses the negation frame to park at the boundary between imagining and doing:

> *"Imagine the intention to lift."*
> — Jeff Haller

Not "imagine lifting." Imagine the *intention* to lift. This is exquisitely precise — the student finds the pre-motor spark, the moment before movement begins, and stays there.

**Agency attribution** gives body parts their own personality, intention, or perception:

> *"The knee has an eye on it, and it's seeing a place that it's aiming towards."*
> — FM training teacher

When the knee "sees," the student's motor planning shifts from central command ("I move my knee") to distributed organization ("the knee finds its way"). The body part becomes an agent rather than an instrument.

Feldenkrais used this with characteristic irony:

> *"The foot just laughs at you."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

The personification is confrontational — the foot has opinions about how you're using it, and it finds your efforts amusing. This creates a relationship between the student and their foot that's more vivid than "pay attention to your foot."

Gibbons takes a different approach:

> *"The arm is innocent."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

"Innocent" — meaning the arm isn't the source of the effort or the problem. This personification redirects the student's attention away from the arm and toward whatever *is* doing the work. It's a constraint disguised as a metaphor.

**Listen for this**: When a teacher uses an image, ask yourself: is it organizing a movement (how the body should move), organizing a state (how the experience should feel), or organizing the person's relationship to a body part (who is doing what)? Each type works through a different pathway.

**Try this**: Take a movement you teach — say, rolling from back to side. Teach it three ways: with a movement-organizing image ("pour yourself like water flowing down a hillside"), with imagination ("without moving, imagine rolling — feel which parts would leave the floor first"), and with agency attribution ("let your shoulder lead — it knows where it wants to go, just follow it"). Notice which version produces the most fluid, least effortful movement.

### Cluster 6: What You Reveal

#### Pedagogical Transparency + Environmental Animism

Some of the most powerful moments in somatic teaching come when the teacher steps back from instruction and speaks about the *purpose* of what they're doing — or when they animate the environment itself as a participant in the lesson.

**Pedagogical transparency** means explaining *why* you're asking what you're asking. This shifts the student from obedience to understanding.

Gibbons, teaching a lesson on breathing, tells his students:

> *"The approach is not to teach you the correct way to breathe... We teach all kinds of ways of breathing."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

In one sentence, he dismantles the student's assumption that there's a right answer. This reframes the entire lesson: it's not about achieving correct breathing but about expanding the repertoire of possible breathings. The student who hears this engages with each variation as an experiment rather than a test.

Feldenkrais names the core principle his lesson is built on:

> *"Anything we can't do, we substitute something else for it."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

This is his substitution theory — the idea that when a movement path is unavailable, the nervous system routes around it, and we confuse the substitution for the real thing. By naming this principle, Feldenkrais gives the student a framework for everything that follows. Each movement becomes an investigation: am I doing this, or substituting?

Clark explains what kind of attention the lesson requires:

> *"Trying to reach the parts of ourselves that are actually in control... something far from consciousness."*
> — Scott Clark

This is a remarkable instruction. It tells the student that what they need to attend to is, by definition, not readily available to consciousness. It prepares them for the disorientation of not being able to feel what the teacher is asking about — and reframes that inability as expected, not as failure.

Strauss-Klein uses transparency to create safety:

> *"Our self-image is very personal to us."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This acknowledges that a lesson that changes how someone feels their body is touching something intimate. The acknowledgment itself creates permission.

Pedagogical transparency is more prominent in training contexts, where students are learning to teach, than in public classes. But even in a public lesson, a single sentence of rationale can transform the student's engagement from following instructions to conducting experiments.

**Environmental animism** treats the floor, gravity, and the room as active participants rather than passive surfaces.

Jeff Haller's language consistently animates the environment:

> *"The floor invites you into a different story."*
> — Jeff Haller

The floor "invites" — it has agency, intention, something to offer. This is not decoration. When the surface is animated, the student's muscular organization shifts. Instead of *pushing against* the floor, they begin *receiving from* it. The reorganization from push to receive is one of the great transformations in somatic education, and it often starts with a single word: "invites."

Strauss-Klein makes gravity into a resource:

> *"Imagine the floor pushing you up."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This inverts the usual relationship. Students are accustomed to pressing down against the floor. The image of the floor pushing up changes the direction of their effort — or rather, removes effort by providing a sense of support from below.

Training teachers describe force as something that radiates through the body:

> *"The pressure is distributed from a particular point and then from there."*
> — FM training teacher

Force doesn't just press — it travels, spreads, distributes. This language creates a sense of the body as a network of pathways rather than a collection of parts.

Not all teachers animate the environment. Clark and Schmid-Blackeby treat the floor as a passive reference — a surface to sense weight against, but not a partner. This works too. Environmental animism is a stylistic choice, not a requirement.

**Listen for this**: Does the teacher talk about the floor, gravity, or the room? If so, is the environment passive ("feel the floor under you") or active ("let the floor support you")? The difference is subtle but consequential.

**Try this**: In your next scan at the beginning of a lesson, instead of "Feel where your body contacts the floor," try "Feel where the floor meets your body — where it rises to support you, where there are gaps." Notice whether this changes the quality of the room.

## Part II: Lesson Architecture

The mechanisms from Part I are the vocabulary. Now we turn to grammar — the recurring structural patterns that organize these tools into coherent lessons. If the mechanisms are what a teacher says, the patterns are the shape of when and why they say it.

### The Awareness Sandwich

This is the most universal pattern in somatic teaching, found in 17 of 19 lessons we studied:

1. **Scan** — sense your current state (using naming, sensory channels, questions)
2. **Explore** — try movements (using verb frames, constraints, images)
3. **Rest** — pause to integrate (silence)
4. **Rescan** — sense what changed (the same questions as step 1, but the answers are different)

The scan/rescan pair is the structural heart of a Feldenkrais lesson. It's what makes the learning *visible to the student*. Without it, changes happen but the student may not notice them. With it, the student has a before-and-after comparison that demonstrates, in their own felt experience, that something shifted.

Here's how it works in practice. At the beginning of a lesson, the teacher might say: *"Lie on your back. Feel the contact of your body with the floor. Which side feels heavier? Is there more weight on the right shoulder blade or the left? Notice the distance between your lower back and the floor."* This is the scan. The student takes stock.

Then comes the exploration — twenty or thirty minutes of movements, constraints, questions, images. All the tools from Part I, deployed in service of a movement theme.

Then a rest. The student lies still. Nothing is said. The nervous system integrates.

Then: *"Feel the contact of your body with the floor again. Which side feels heavier now? How does the distance between your lower back and the floor compare to before?"* The same questions. But the student's body has changed, and the answers are different.

The power is in using the *same* questions in both the scan and the rescan. "Which side feels longer?" at the beginning might yield "my right side." After twenty minutes of exploration, the same question might yield "my left side" — or "they feel equal" — or "I'm not sure anymore, everything feels different." Whatever the answer, the student knows something changed *because they have the comparison*.

Multiple lessons nest several Awareness Sandwiches within a larger one: a big scan at the start, several explore-rest-minirescan cycles, and a big rescan at the end. The mini-scans track local changes; the big scan/rescan tracks the cumulative shift. The nesting is particularly clear in Haller's lessons, where brief rests punctuate each new exploration — the student accumulates a sense of progressive change, each mini-rescan adding to the evidence.

The two lessons in our corpus that lacked the Awareness Sandwich had unusual structures — one was more lecture-demonstration than exploration, the other more meditative. The pattern is nearly universal because it does something no other structure does: it gives the student *proof* that they changed. Not the teacher's word for it. Their own felt experience.

**Listen for this**: Where does the scan happen? How specific are the questions? A teacher who asks "How do you feel?" in the scan has given the student nothing to compare. A teacher who asks "Feel the weight on your left sit bone compared to your right" has given them a precise benchmark.

### The Constraint Ladder

Most lessons progressively vary constraints to reveal different aspects of the same movement theme. Think of it as turning a gem slowly — each rotation catches the light differently, but it's the same gem.

This takes several forms:

**Progressive narrowing** moves from global to specific. The teacher might start with a whole-body movement — rolling from back to side — then focus on just the pelvis, then on the relationship between pelvis and shoulder, then on the specific moment where the pelvis begins to lead the shoulder. Each step narrows the attentional field, revealing finer distinctions.

**Progressive integration** teaches parts separately, then combines them. Schmid-Blackeby's characteristic approach: arm movement alone, leg movement alone, then arm and leg together with a timing constraint — *"Lift your head and your foot at precisely the same moment."* The student who could easily do each part alone suddenly faces a coordination challenge that reveals how their whole body is organized.

**Progressive complexity** keeps the same theme but adds constraint after constraint. One Feldenkrais Manhattan lesson builds from a simple lift to swinging, reaching, grasping, opening and closing the hand — all organized around the spatial clock face. The student pushes their foot toward twelve o'clock, then three o'clock, then six, accumulating a spatial map. Each step adds a new demand on the same underlying organization, like adding voices to a musical round.

**Orientation tour** takes the same movement through different body positions: lying on your back, then your stomach, then your side, then sitting. Gibbons uses this extensively. A rotation that feels easy on your back becomes puzzling on your stomach — because the relationship to gravity changes, and the student must reorganize entirely. Each position reveals what the previous one concealed.

The common thread: **systematic variation of constraints reveals different aspects of the same movement theme**. The teacher isn't teaching a sequence of different movements — they're showing the student different facets of one movement, each facet made visible by a different constraint.

**Listen for this**: When a new constraint is introduced, does it reveal something the previous constraint couldn't? In a well-crafted Constraint Ladder, each step creates a new "aha" — a new discovery about the same underlying movement.

### Component-then-Assembly

Teach parts separately, then combine:

1. Teach the arm movement alone
2. Teach the leg movement alone
3. Combine arm + leg with a timing constraint
4. Add head, breath, or other components

This differs from the Constraint Ladder because the student learns each component *before* combining them, rather than progressively restricting a whole movement. It's particularly common in lessons involving bilateral or multi-limb coordination.

Schmid-Blackeby uses this pattern most distinctively. In her lessons, each component gets thorough attention — the arm movement is explored fully, with its own constraints and sensory questions, before the leg enters. Then the leg gets equal treatment. Only then comes the combination.

The assembly moment — when separate components come together — is often where the most dramatic learning happens. Students who could easily do each part alone find the combination surprisingly difficult, which reveals how much their habitual organization depends on isolating movements. The difficulty *is* the lesson: it shows the student how their nervous system works.

A training teacher at Feldenkrais Manhattan uses a variant: *"Organize it in your imagination. Get the order sensed and then go ahead."* Here, the "assembly" happens first in imagination, then in action — the student mentally rehearses the combination before attempting it physically.

**Listen for this**: When components come together, does the teacher acknowledge the difficulty? The best teachers expect the assembly to be hard — and frame the difficulty as information, not failure.

### The Effort Reduction Arc

Some lessons follow a trajectory from more effort to less:

1. Move with normal effort
2. Make the movement smaller
3. Make it lighter
4. Imagine the movement without doing it
5. Do the movement again — notice the changed quality

The language shifts along this arc: from imperative ("lift your arm") to invitation ("allow your arm to float") to conditional ("what happens if the arm begins to rise") to imaginal ("without moving, imagine the arm rising"). The verbal frame mirrors the effort trajectory — as the movement gets smaller and lighter, the language gets softer and more open.

Jeff Haller's Proportional Lifting lesson demonstrates this beautifully. The lesson begins with actual lifting — the student engages with effort, weight, the mechanics of rising. As the lesson progresses, the movements become smaller, the questions more about quality than quantity, and eventually Haller asks the student to "imagine the intention to lift" — to find the pre-motor spark without executing the movement. By the end, the student stands with a quality of effortless height that no amount of instruction could produce.

Strauss-Klein's Anti-Gravity lesson follows a similar arc, with his characteristic warmth: the student gradually discovers that "up" is not something you push toward but something you allow.

But the Effort Reduction Arc is not universal — many lessons maintain a consistent effort level throughout. Clark's lessons refine attention without reducing effort. Schmid-Blackeby's rolling lessons maintain a consistent physical demand while varying the coordination challenge. The Effort Reduction Arc is a common and effective structural choice, not a requirement. Some teachers believe that reducing effort is the path to learning; others believe that refining attention within consistent effort is equally powerful.

**Listen for this**: Does effort decrease across the lesson? Does the language shift from imperative to invitational? If both happen together, you're hearing the Effort Reduction Arc in action.

## Part III: What Goes Wrong

Every one of the mechanisms described above can be misused. But not all errors are equal. The most important distinction is between language that **interrupts embodied attention** (always harmful) and language that **reframes the meaning of embodied attention** (sometimes powerful).

### Always Harmful

**Vague sensory language** fails because it gives the nervous system nothing to organize around. Compare:

| Before (vague) | After (specific) |
|----------------|-----------------|
| *"Be aware of your body."* | *"Feel the weight of your head on the floor. Is there more weight on the right side or the left?"* |
| *"Notice how that feels."* | *"Notice whether the movement travels into your ribs — and if so, which ribs."* |
| *"Relax into the floor."* | *"Let the back of your left shoulder blade settle toward the floor. Does it touch evenly, or is there a part that hovers?"* |

The vague versions give the student no entry point. "Be aware of your body" is like saying "listen to the music" without specifying melody, rhythm, or harmony. The specific versions activate a precise sensory search — the student must locate a body part, attend to a quality (weight, pressure, distance), and make a distinction (right vs. left, even vs. uneven). The specificity *is* the instruction.

Across all 19 lessons studied, effective teachers were consistently specific. When language was vague, it was either a momentary lapse or — rarely — an intentional "open container" that trusted the student to fill in their own content. The difference between lazy vagueness and intentional openness is whether the teacher has thought about what they're directing attention to. When Feldenkrais says *"this experience of yourself"* — deliberately open — he's trusting each student to find their own focus. When a beginning teacher says "notice how you feel," they usually haven't decided what the student should attend to.

**Corrective framing** activates judgment rather than curiosity:

| Before (corrective) | After (exploratory) |
|---------------------|---------------------|
| *"Don't tense your shoulders."* | *"What are your shoulders doing right now?"* |
| *"Your pelvis should be neutral."* | *"Where is your pelvis? Is it tilted forward, back, or somewhere else?"* |
| *"You're holding your breath — breathe."* | *"What is your breathing doing during this movement?"* |
| *"Keep your foot relaxed."* | *"How much effort is in your foot? Could you do this with a softer foot?"* |

The corrective versions create a right/wrong frame. The student who hears "don't tense your shoulders" starts monitoring for tension — which is itself a form of tension. The exploratory versions create the same awareness without judgment. A student who notices their own shoulder tension will adjust it themselves, and the adjustment will be organic rather than imposed.

The corrective impulse is strong, especially for new teachers. You see something you want to change and you name it as wrong. But in somatic education, the act of noticing — without judgment — *is* the correction. The question does the work that the correction tried to force.

### Context-Dependent

These are trickier. The same linguistic choice can interrupt learning or deepen it, depending on how it's used.

**Biomechanical explanation during movement** interrupts when it *replaces* movement attention — when the student shifts from sensing to thinking about anatomy. But it deepens learning when it gives the student a framework for what they're sensing.

| Interrupts learning | Deepens learning |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| *"The iliopsoas connects the lumbar vertebrae to the lesser trochanter of the femur and functions as the primary hip flexor"* (during a movement — the student goes into their head) | *"The muscle you're feeling connects deep inside, from your lower back all the way down to the inner thigh — can you sense that length?"* (maps an anatomical fact onto a sensory experience) |
| *"Breathe diaphragmatically"* (prescriptive, no sensory anchor) | *"The approach is not to teach you the correct way to breathe... We teach all kinds of ways of breathing"* — Gibbons (reframes the purpose) |

Gibbons frequently explains the biomechanical rationale for a constraint: why the shoulders are staying on the floor, what the pelvis is doing relative to the ribcage, how the arm movement relates to the spine. This explanation doesn't interrupt because it sends the student *further into* their body rather than *out of* it. The key is whether the student can use the information sensorily. "Your T12 is the pivot point" is useful if the student can feel T12; otherwise, it's a trip into the intellectual realm.

Feldenkrais himself lectured extensively during movement — about substitution, about habit, about the gap between intention and action. But his lectures deepened the student's relationship to what they were discovering. He explained *why* they couldn't do what he asked, which made the inability more interesting rather than more frustrating.

The test: after the explanation, is the student attending more closely to their body, or less? If the explanation sends them further in, keep going. If their eyes glaze over, stop.

**Outcome description** tells the student what to feel rather than letting them discover it:

| Interrupts learning | Deepens learning |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| *"Now you should feel more relaxed."* (tells the student what to feel — creates performance pressure) | *"Has anything changed? What do you notice?"* (lets the student discover) |
| *"The right side should feel longer."* (prescribes the experience) | *"Our bodies are organized to make this possible — can you find how?"* — Haller (outcome as hypothesis) |
| *"You'll feel more balanced when you stand."* (predicts the future) | *"Stand up slowly and walk around. Let yourself be surprised."* (invites discovery) |

The student who hears "you should feel more relaxed" and doesn't feel more relaxed now has two problems: whatever brought them to the lesson, plus the anxiety that they're doing it wrong. The student who hears "what do you notice?" has permission to discover whatever is actually there.

Haller's approach is particularly elegant:

> *"Our bodies are organized to make this possible — can you find how?"*
> — Jeff Haller

The outcome becomes a hypothesis to test, not a benchmark to meet. "Our bodies are organized to make this possible" is a statement of faith in the student's nervous system. "Can you find how?" hands the investigation back to them.

**Over-cuing (continuous talking without silence)** prevents self-organization when it fills space, but guides attention when every word serves a purpose:

| Interrupts learning | Deepens learning |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| Talking continuously through a rest period, unable to tolerate silence | Dense verbal guidance that builds a spatial map: *"Feel the first rib... now the second... the third... can you feel where the rib meets the vertebra?"* |
| Narrating what the student is already doing: *"You're rolling to the side, your arm is coming along..."* | Maintaining a quality through a complex coordination: *"Keep the lightness as you add the leg... same quality, just more of you participating"* |

Gibbons's rib-palpation sections, where he guides the student through feeling each rib in sequence, are among the densest verbal passages in the corpus — and also among the most effective. Every word directs attention rather than filling silence. The student moves through a detailed sensory landscape, guided by language that's dense because the terrain is complex, not because the teacher is nervous.

The test: if you removed the talking, would the student lose something important? If yes, the density is earned. If the student would actually benefit from silence, the density is a habit.

### The Feldenkrais Exception

Moshe Feldenkrais violated nearly every anti-pattern. He lectured during movement, corrected students publicly, and used prescriptive framing. But his teaching was effective because the violations served a specific pedagogical purpose:

His lecturing was *about* the gap between intention and action — it deepened the student's discovery rather than replacing it.

His corrections demonstrated the substitution principle: *"Look at the difference between what you think you're doing and what you're actually doing."* This wasn't "do it right" — it was "notice that you can't do what you intend."

His prescriptive framing was not "do it correctly" but "notice that you can't do it." This is a confrontational style that makes the student's own confusion the lesson material.

> *"Your heel is motionless completely."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais, correcting a student publicly

In a contemporary class, this would feel harsh. In Feldenkrais's hands, it was a revelation — the student discovers a part of themselves that isn't participating in the movement, and that discovery becomes the doorway to change.

The lesson: anti-patterns are harmful when they interrupt embodied attention. They can be effective when they reframe the *meaning* of embodied attention. But deploying them this way requires deep pedagogical skill — and if you're not sure, the safer choice is always to ask a question instead of making a statement.

## Part IV: Five Ways to Teach the Same Thing

The deepest finding of this study is not about mechanisms or patterns — it's about why teachers differ. Each teacher in our corpus uses the same tools, but deploys them in service of a different *theory of how learning happens*. The tools are neutral. The teaching philosophy determines everything.

### Jeff Haller: Change the Image, Change the Movement

Haller teaches through body image transformation. His core belief: if the student's internal picture of how they're organized in space changes, the movement follows automatically. You don't need to teach the movement — you need to teach the image.

His primary tools are spatial-perceptual imagery, anti-gravitational framing, and questions about effort. The floor is a partner, gravity is a resource, and the student's job is to update their internal map.

His language is warm, invitational, rich with images:

> *"The floor invites you into a different story."*
> — Jeff Haller

This isn't a throwaway line. It captures Haller's entire approach: the floor has something to offer, and the student's job is to be available to receive it. Compare this with "Feel the floor under you" — which treats the floor as passive. Haller's floor is an active participant in the lesson.

He asks questions about effort that guide the student toward less:

> *"Our bodies are organized to make this possible — can you find how?"*
> — Jeff Haller

The word "find" is doing critical work here. It implies the student already has what they need — they just haven't uncovered it yet. And when the student does find it, the discovery belongs to them, not the teacher.

In his Proportional Lifting lesson, Haller guides students from actual lifting to imagining the intention to lift — and by the end, the student stands with a quality of effortless height that came from updating the body image, not from muscular training.

A Haller lesson feels like slowly dissolving into a more comfortable relationship with gravity. By the end, the student stands differently not because they were told how to stand, but because they feel the floor differently.

### Andrew Gibbons: Understand the Mechanics, Free the Movement

Gibbons teaches through mechanical understanding. His core belief: when the student understands *why* a movement is organized as it is — anatomically, biomechanically, pedagogically — the movement becomes available. Understanding frees.

His primary tools are anatomical naming, constraint rationale, meta-pedagogical commentary, and surprisingly poetic verb frames. He is the most intellectually explicit teacher in the study — and one of the most effective, because his explanations serve the body rather than replacing it.

He names anatomy with precision:

> *"The anterior superior iliac spine."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

He tells students why he's imposing a constraint:

> *"The approach is not to teach you the correct way to breathe... We teach all kinds of ways of breathing."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

He uses constraints more polymorphically than any other teacher — positional, negative, decoupling, comparative:

> *"Hold your breath and continue the movement without breathing."*
> *"Do NOT turn the pelvis."*
> *"Same rotation, but now the pelvis moves equally with the chest."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

And then, unexpectedly, he deploys the most literary language in the corpus:

> *"Pour yourself to the side."*
> *"Seduce yourself into comfort."*
> — Andrew Gibbons

The range — from anatomical precision to "seduce yourself into comfort" — captures something essential about Gibbons: he believes that understanding and sensory poetry are not opposites. The student who understands the mechanics *and* can "pour" themselves to the side has the fullest possible relationship with the movement.

A Gibbons lesson feels like a guided investigation. The student comes away not just sensing differently but *understanding* why they sense differently — and being able to explain it to someone else.

### Nick Strauss-Klein: Create the Conditions, Trust the Nervous System

Strauss-Klein teaches through creating conditions for self-organization. His core belief: if you provide safety, curiosity, permission, and the right conditions, the nervous system will organize itself. The teacher's job is to create the container, not to fill it.

His primary tools are questions, permissions, warm imagery, and imagination. His language creates spaciousness:

> *"Our self-image is very personal to us."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This acknowledges — before the lesson even begins — that what they're about to do touches something intimate. The acknowledgment itself creates safety.

> *"Imagine the floor pushing you up."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

His images are consistently warm and permissive. The floor doesn't just support — it actively *helps*. Gravity isn't a force to fight — it's a resource to receive.

His most extraordinary teaching moment in the corpus: in a lesson on the carriage of the head, after teaching the right side through movement, he teaches the entire left side through imagination alone. The students don't physically move their left side at all — they imagine the movements they did on the right. When they finally move the left side physically, it has changed. The imagination was the lesson.

> *"Wait for the need to breathe to grow on its own."*
> — Nick Strauss-Klein

This instruction asks the student to do the hardest thing in somatic education: nothing. Wait. Don't manufacture the breath — let the body decide when it's needed. The trust this requires — in the nervous system, in the process, in the body's intelligence — is the essence of Strauss-Klein's approach.

A Strauss-Klein lesson feels like being given permission to be curious about yourself. The learning happens not because the teacher directed it, but because the teacher created an environment where it could emerge.

### Scott Clark: Direct Attention Precisely, Let Sensation Emerge

Clark teaches through radical attentional precision. His core belief: if you direct attention precisely enough — to exactly the right place, at exactly the right scale — sensation emerges on its own. You don't need images, explanations, or even movement. You need exquisite attention.

His primary tools are existential questions, naming (including naming absence), and compressed instruction. He uses almost no imagery and no explanatory framework. His language is the sparsest in the study:

> *"How do you know that hand is present?"*
> — Scott Clark

This question doesn't ask what the student feels. It asks how they *know* that they feel. It addresses the ground of experience itself — not sensation, but the awareness of sensation. This is the deepest level a somatic question can reach, and Clark goes there routinely.

His willingness to name absence is equally distinctive:

> *"For years I would have had a hard time swearing in a court of law that I had a neck."*
> — Scott Clark

Most teachers direct attention to what's there. Clark names what's *not* there — and in doing so, validates every student who also can't feel their neck. The absence becomes the starting point, not the problem.

He explains what his lesson requires — not in biomechanical terms, but in attentional terms:

> *"Trying to reach the parts of ourselves that are actually in control... something far from consciousness."*
> — Scott Clark

This prepares the student for a specific kind of frustration: what they need to attend to is, by definition, not readily available. The warning reframes confusion as expected rather than as failure.

A Clark lesson feels like the world getting quieter. The student becomes attentive to subtleties they normally rush past — the ground of experience itself, rather than what's happening on the surface.

### Moshe Feldenkrais: Confront the Gap Between Intention and Action

Feldenkrais teaches through confrontation. His core belief: the most important thing a person can discover is the gap between what they *intend* to do and what they *actually* do. Everything else — flexibility, coordination, comfort — follows from closing that gap.

His primary tools are provocation, philosophical argument, public demonstration, and the imperative verb frame. His approach is the most challenging in the study — and the most historically influential.

His language is commanding, ironic, sometimes confrontational:

> *"Your heel is motionless completely."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais, correcting a student publicly

In a contemporary class, this would feel harsh. In Feldenkrais's hands, it was a revelation — the student discovers a part of themselves that isn't participating in the movement. The correction doesn't prescribe what to do; it reveals what *isn't happening*.

He personifies body parts with characteristic irony:

> *"The foot just laughs at you."*
> *"Who is the heel? What sort of authority is that?"*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

The foot that laughs has its own intelligence, separate from the student's intention. The heel that is a "sort of authority" reframes the relationship between self and body part — from command-and-control to negotiation. These aren't whimsical asides; they're philosophical provocations embedded in movement instruction.

He names the principle his lesson is built on:

> *"Anything we can't do, we substitute something else for it."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

And then he demonstrates this in the student's own body — showing them that what they think they're doing and what they're actually doing are not the same thing.

He uses developmental naming to give the body a history:

> *"The first contact with the outside world is the mouth."*
> *"The mouth is the most conscious part of ourselves."*
> — Moshe Feldenkrais

The mouth isn't just an anatomical structure — it's the organ through which the infant first met the world. By invoking this developmental significance, Feldenkrais gives the student's attention to their mouth a different quality — not clinical, but archaeological. They're reconnecting with something very old.

A Feldenkrais lesson feels like being simultaneously challenged and respected. The teacher knows more than you about your own body — and he's going to prove it — but the proof is always in your own experience.

### A Note on Historical and Contemporary Style

One dimension of difference deserves separate attention: the historical shift from Feldenkrais's 1970s style to contemporary practice.

| Dimension | Feldenkrais | Contemporary teachers |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Verb frame** | Imperative dominant | Invitational dominant |
| **Correction** | Direct, public | Absent or indirect |
| **Theory** | Integrated into instruction | Separated or meta-commented |
| **Humor** | Ironic, confrontational | Self-deprecating, warm |
| **Student agency** | Teacher-directed | Student-empowered |
| **Treatment of failure** | Confrontation (*"the foot laughs at you"*) | Normalization (*"it's a sign of intelligence to rest"*) |

This is not a story of progress from bad to good. Feldenkrais's confrontational style was effective because it served a specific purpose — making the gap between intention and action vivid enough to be unavoidable. Contemporary invitational styles serve a different purpose — creating safety for self-organization. Both work. The shift reflects changing pedagogical values, not a discovery that one approach is better.

### The Takeaway

None of these approaches is "correct." They are five different answers to the same question: *What does a person need in order to learn about themselves through movement?*

Haller says: a new image. Gibbons says: understanding. Strauss-Klein says: safety. Clark says: precise attention. Feldenkrais says: confrontation with reality.

Each answer leads to different language choices, different emphasis among the twelve mechanisms, different lesson structures. And each produces genuine learning — different in quality, but equally real.

The most valuable thing a developing practitioner can do is not to adopt one style, but to understand *why* different styles work. When you know why Jeff Haller animates the floor, you can choose whether to animate it — based on what your student needs, not on what you've been trained to do.

## Closing: Developing Your Ear

The twelve mechanisms and four structural patterns in this booklet are not a checklist. They're a way of hearing — a perceptual framework that lets you listen to somatic teaching with new ears.

Here's how to practice:

**Stage 1: Notice the mechanisms.** Listen to a lesson (your own teaching, a recording, a live class) and simply name what you hear. "That was a naming instruction. That was an invitation frame. That was a comparison question. There — a constraint." Don't evaluate. Just notice. This alone will sharpen your teaching within weeks, because awareness precedes change.

**Stage 2: Notice the patterns.** Zoom out from individual instructions to the lesson's architecture. Where's the first scan? When does the exploration begin? Is there a constraint ladder? Do the constraints build, or does the teacher repeat the same level? Where are the rests? Is there a rescan?

**Stage 3: Notice the philosophy.** Ask: what does this teacher believe about how people learn? Are they trying to change the student's body image? Build understanding? Create safety? Direct attention? Confront the gap between intention and action? The answer will explain most of their language choices.

**Stage 4: Notice your own defaults.** We all have a home territory — a favorite verb frame, a preferred sensory channel, a habitual constraint type. There's nothing wrong with defaults, but knowing what yours are gives you the option to step outside them. If you always invite, try commanding. If you always constrain, try asking a question. If you never use imagery, try one image and see what happens.

**Stage 5: Listen to your students.** Not to their words — to their movement quality. When you change your language, does the movement change? More fluid? More careful? More confused? More exploratory? The movement will tell you whether your language is working. If the movement doesn't change, the language didn't land.

The point of all this is not to become a linguist. It's to become a more deliberate teacher — one who chooses words not from habit but from understanding. Language is your instrument. The twelve mechanisms are its range. Your teaching philosophy is the music you choose to play.

The people who do this work — who use language to help others discover how they move and sense and breathe — are doing something remarkable. They're using the most human tool we have (words) to access the most fundamental human experience (the body). The better we understand how that works, the better we can do it.

Now go listen to a lesson. Hear what the words are doing. And then say something to your students — deliberately, precisely, with full awareness of the instrument in your hands.
