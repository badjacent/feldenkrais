# Somatic Language: Seed Theory (v2 — Post-Corpus Evaluation)

A theory of how language creates embodied experience. Derived from linguistic-somatic research, Feldenkrais pedagogy, and phenomenological analysis of movement instruction. Phase 1 focuses on **cueing language** and **sensory language** — the two dimensions where verbal choices most directly affect what happens in a person's body.

> **v2 changes**: After evaluating 19 lessons across 7 speaker/source groups, we added 4 new mechanisms (9–12), 2 new compositional patterns (D–E), refined 4 existing mechanisms, and nuanced the anti-pattern section. Changes are marked with [v2].

## Core Premise

Language in somatic education is not description — it is *action*. The words a teacher chooses do not merely convey information about a movement; they shape the quality, speed, attention, and felt sense of the movement itself. A skilled practitioner treats language as their primary instrument.

[v2] **Addendum**: Different teachers deploy the same mechanisms with different underlying theories of how learning happens. Haller teaches through body image transformation; Gibbons through mechanical understanding; Strauss-Klein through conditions for self-organization; Clark through precise attention; Feldenkrais through confrontation with the gap between intention and action. The mechanisms are tools; the teacher's implicit learning theory determines how they are wielded.

## I. Mechanisms: How Language Moves a Body

### 1. Naming Creates Presence
**Claim**: When a teacher names a body part, the student's nervous system increases its representation of that part — it becomes more present in awareness.

- "Feel your left sit bone" does not just direct attention — it *wakes up* the sensory map of that area
- The effect is stronger for parts that are usually below awareness (sit bones, inner ribs, tongue root) than for familiar parts (hands, face)
- **Implication**: The order in which a teacher names body parts creates a sequence of awakening — this is a compositional tool, not just logistics

[v2] **Corpus evidence**: Universal across all 19 lessons. Takes several forms:
- Anatomical naming: "the anterior superior iliac spine" (Gibbons)
- Spatial naming: "the exact place where the head touches your mat" (FM teacher)
- Developmental naming: "the first contact with the outside world is the mouth" (Feldenkrais)
- Significance naming: "the mouth is the most conscious part of ourselves" (Feldenkrais)
- Naming absence: "for years I would have had a hard time swearing in a court of law that I had a neck" (Clark) — naming the *difficulty* of finding presence

**Confidence**: High — confirmed across all speaker groups.

### 2. Verb Frame Shapes Movement Quality
**Claim**: The grammatical and semantic frame of the movement verb determines the quality of the resulting movement.

| Frame | Example | Effect |
|-------|---------|--------|
| **Imperative/direct** | "Lift your arm" | Activates voluntary motor planning. Efficient but may engage habitual patterns. |
| **Invitation** | "Allow your arm to float upward" | Engages parasympathetic tone. Reduces effort. Movement emerges rather than being executed. |
| **Conditional** | "What happens if your arm begins to rise?" | Shifts to inquiry mode. Student becomes observer of their own movement. |
| **Negation** | "Without lifting your arm, imagine the intention to lift" | Dissociates intention from action. Activates motor planning without execution (ideomotor effect). |
| **Passive/agentless** | "The arm rises" | Removes the student as agent. Can create a sense of effortlessness or disorientation. |
| [v2] **Discovery** | "Find the support through your skeleton" | Implies the organization already exists, waiting to be found. |
| [v2] **Literary/aesthetic** | "Pour yourself to the side" / "Seduce yourself into comfort" | Recruits aesthetic sensibility as a movement organizer. Rare but powerful. |

- The progression from imperative → invitation → conditional → negation often maps to a lesson's pedagogical arc: from doing → sensing → differentiating → integrating
- Skilled teachers shift frame within a lesson, often within a single exploration
- [v2] Historical note: Feldenkrais used predominantly imperative frames; contemporary teachers favor invitational frames. The shift reflects changing pedagogical values, not a discovery that invitation works better.

**Confidence**: High — confirmed across all speaker groups. The table of frames is well-supported; the claim that frame progression maps to pedagogical arc is partially supported (some lessons maintain a consistent frame throughout).

### 3. Temporal Framing Modulates Speed and Attention
**Claim**: The tense and temporal markers in an instruction directly affect the pace and attentional quality of movement.

| Temporal frame | Example | Effect |
|----------------|---------|--------|
| **Present continuous** | "You are turning your head to the right" | Concurrent narration. Student tracks movement in real time. |
| **Beginning/onset** | "Begin to turn your head" | Focus on initiation. Student attends to the very start of movement. |
| **Progressive** | "Slowly, slowly let the turning continue" | Extends duration. Draws attention to the middle of movement. |
| **Completion** | "And come to rest" | Marks an endpoint. Invites settling and integration. |
| **Before-the-movement** | "Before you move, sense..." | Pre-movement awareness. Creates a gap between intention and action. |
| [v2] **Simultaneity** | "Lift your head and your foot at precisely the same moment" | Coordination demand. Forces global organization. |
| [v2] **Temporal patience** | "Wait for the need to breathe to grow on its own" | Extended waiting. Creates receptivity rather than activity. |

- "Begin to..." is one of the most powerful phrases in somatic teaching — it directs attention to the threshold between stillness and movement, where the most learning happens
- Skilled teachers use temporal framing to stretch time, making 5 seconds of movement feel like a minute of experience
- [v2] Timing-as-constraint: Schmid-Blackeby makes temporal coordination (arm and leg moving simultaneously) the primary learning variable, not the movement itself

**Confidence**: High — confirmed across all speaker groups.

### 4. Sensory Channel Selection
**Claim**: The sensory modality invoked by language determines which neural pathways participate in the movement.

| Channel | Language markers | Neural effect |
|---------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Proprioceptive** | "Feel the weight... sense the pressure... notice the contact" | Body-position processing |
| [v2] **Muscular identification** | "Which side of the back of the waist do you feel contracts?" | Specific muscle-group detection (distinct from general proprioception) |
| **Visual** | "See your hand moving... imagine watching from above" | Visual cortex engagement, external perspective |
| **Spatial** | "Toward the ceiling... away from your spine... the distance between" | Spatial processing, relational awareness |
| **Effort/force** | "How much effort... lighter... softer... with less push" | Motor cortex modulation, inhibition |
| [v2] **Force-vector** | "Where does the push go through your body?" | Imagined force direction — a spatial-kinesthetic hybrid |
| [v2] **Quantitative** | "How many inches are you lifting? Is it five? Is it ten?" | Numerical sensing creates a different quality of attention than qualitative |
| [v2] **Interoceptive** | "Sense the pulse... the warmth... the vibration" | Internal-state awareness (pulse, temperature, oscillation) |
| **Temperature/texture** | "Warm... smooth... as if moving through water" | Interoceptive, metaphorical processing |

- Shifting channels within an exploration creates richer neural mapping — a movement that has been "seen," "felt," and "measured" is more fully learned
- Different students respond to different channels; skilled teachers offer multiple entry points
- [v2] **Corpus finding**: Temperature/texture channel was absent from all 19 lessons. The theory overweighted this channel based on non-Feldenkrais somatic traditions. It may be more prominent in somatics that use water, contact, or guided touch.
- [v2] **Corpus finding**: The dominant channels in practice are proprioceptive, spatial, and effort/force. Visual is used rarely (primarily Strauss-Klein's eye-hand lesson).

**Confidence**: High for the general principle; individual channel descriptions refined based on corpus. Temperature/texture downgraded to Hypothesis for Feldenkrais specifically.

### 5. The Constraint-as-Teacher Principle
**Claim**: Language that introduces constraints (physical or attentional) creates learning more effectively than language that describes the desired outcome.

- "Keep your shoulders on the floor as you roll your pelvis" teaches the student to differentiate pelvis from shoulders — not by explaining differentiation, but by creating conditions where it must occur
- "Notice what your ribs do when you turn your head" makes the student discover a connection rather than being told about it
- **The teacher's job is to create constraints that make the desired organization the easiest path** — language is the primary tool for this

[v2] **Corpus evidence**: The most polymorphic mechanism — takes many more forms than originally described:

| Constraint type | Example | Source |
|----------------|---------|--------|
| **Positional** | "Keep your shoulders on the floor" | Universal |
| **Attentional** | "Notice what your ribs do" | Universal |
| [v2] **Temporal** | "Arm and leg must move at the same time" | Schmid-Blackeby |
| [v2] **Decoupling** | "Hold your breath and continue the movement without breathing" | Gibbons |
| [v2] **Negative** | "Do NOT turn the pelvis" | Gibbons, Feldenkrais |
| [v2] **Comparative** | "Same rotation, but now the pelvis moves equally with the chest" | Gibbons |
| [v2] **Orientational** | "Now do the same thing on your stomach" | Gibbons, FM |
| [v2] **Quantitative** | "Lift your leg no more than a hover" | OpenATM |
| [v2] **Removal** | "Now remove the constraint and notice what changed" | Universal (reintegration step) |

**Confidence**: High — the most universally supported mechanism. The original description was too narrow; the refined version captures the full polymorphism observed in the corpus.

### 6. The Question as Somatic Tool
**Claim**: Questions addressed to the student's body (not their intellect) create a fundamentally different quality of attention than statements.

- "Is your left hip heavier or lighter than your right?" — the student must attend to both hips simultaneously to answer. The question *creates* the comparison.
- "What happens in your breathing when you do this?" — the student must split attention between the movement and the breath. The question *creates* the connection.
- Questions that cannot be answered intellectually ("How far does this movement travel through your spine?") force sensory engagement.
- Questions with no correct answer ("Which side feels longer?") normalize subjective experience as valid data.

[v2] **Corpus evidence**: Questions serve radically different functions across teachers:

| Question type | Example | Function |
|---------------|---------|----------|
| **Comparison** | "Is your left hip heavier or lighter?" | Creates simultaneous dual attention |
| **Timing/sequence** | "When does your head start moving? When do the ribs start?" | Creates sequential attention |
| **Identification** | "Which side of the waist do you feel contracts?" | Directs to specific muscular recruitment |
| [v2] **Existential** | "How do you know that hand is present?" | Addresses the ground of experience itself |
| [v2] **Philosophical** | "Who is the heel? What sort of authority is that?" | Reframes relationship between self and body part |
| [v2] **Counterfactual** | "Can you imagine what it would feel like if the instructions were given without anything about the pelvis?" | Creates comparison through imagination |
| [v2] **Disturbance-detection** | "Does that tiny movement disturb your breathing?" | Attention to minimal perturbation within continuity |

**Confidence**: High — universal. The sub-types add precision without changing the core claim. Existential questions (Clark) and philosophical questions (Feldenkrais) stretch the mechanism beyond what was originally conceived.

### 7. Image and Metaphor as Organizational Shorthand
**Claim**: Metaphors and images bypass analytical processing and directly organize movement patterns.

- "Let your spine be like a chain" — organizes sequential, segment-by-segment movement without explaining vertebral mechanics
- "Imagine your head is a balloon" — reduces neck tension more effectively than "relax your neck"
- "Pour yourself to the side" — creates fluid, continuous weight shift without any biomechanical vocabulary
- Images work because they are *pre-motor* — they activate a global movement pattern rather than a sequence of muscle commands

**Caution**: Images can also mislead. "Stand tall like a tree" may create rigidity. The skill is choosing images that organize the right pattern.

[v2] **Corpus evidence**: The most variable mechanism across teachers. Distinguished into sub-types:

| Image type | Example | Function |
|------------|---------|----------|
| **Movement-organizing** | "Your spine like a chain" / "the shelf your leg lies on" | Directly shapes motor pattern |
| **State-organizing** | "Like a jellyfish" / "delicious confusion" | Shapes the quality/tone of the experience |
| [v2] **Meaning-organizing narrative** | "Darwin observed children watching apes crack peanuts..." | Creates a conceptual framework for the physical experience |
| [v2] **Developmental recapitulation** | "Have you seen little babies play with their toes against the ground?" | Connects adult movement to infant development |
| [v2] **Structural device** | The clock face (FM Seg 7 Day 8) | An image that organizes an entire lesson's architecture, not just one movement |

[v2] Some teachers (Clark, Feldenkrais, Schmid-Blackeby) use almost no images, relying on direct instruction and questions. Image density is a style variable, not a quality marker.

**Confidence**: High for the general principle. The sub-types are Observed patterns from the corpus.

### 8. Silence and Pauses as Language
**Claim**: The absence of language is itself a somatic instruction — it creates space for self-organization.

- A pause after an instruction gives the nervous system time to process and respond
- Silence after a movement invites integration — the student discovers what changed
- The ratio of instruction to silence is a fundamental teaching variable
- Over-talking prevents learning; under-talking abandons the student

**Confidence**: Observed pattern — universally acknowledged in somatic teaching but impossible to evaluate from transcripts. [v2] Confirmed as impossible to assess: all 19 lessons are transcripts that flatten timing. Multiple teachers *name* rest as important, suggesting awareness of this variable.

### [v2] New Mechanisms (from corpus evaluation)

### 9. Pedagogical Transparency
**Claim**: Language that explains *why* the teacher is asking what they're asking fundamentally changes the student's stance toward the lesson — from obedience to understanding.

- "The approach is not to teach you the correct way to breathe... We teach all kinds of ways of breathing" (Gibbons) — repositions the student's relationship to learning
- "Anything we can't do, we substitute something else for it" (Feldenkrais) — names the principle the lesson is built on
- "Trying to reach the parts of ourselves that are actually in control... something far from consciousness" (Clark) — explains what kind of attention is needed
- The student who knows *why* a constraint is imposed engages with it as an experiment rather than an instruction

**Confidence**: High — found across 4 of 7 speaker groups, spanning historical and contemporary teachers. More prominent in training contexts (where students are learning to teach) than in public classes.

### 10. Environmental Animism
**Claim**: Language that positions the floor, gravity, or the room as active partners rather than passive surfaces changes the student's movement organization.

- "The floor invites you into a different story" (Haller) — the floor has agency
- "Imagine the floor pushing you up" (Strauss-Klein) — gravity becomes a resource
- "The pressure is distributed from a particular point and then from there" (FM teacher) — force radiates, not just presses
- When the surface is animated, the student's muscular organization may shift from "pushing against" to "receiving from"

**Confidence**: Observed pattern — strong in Haller and Strauss-Klein, absent in Clark and Schmid-Blackeby. May be style-specific rather than universal. No independent research confirmation.

### 11. Agency Attribution to Body Parts
**Claim**: Giving body parts their own intention, vision, or personality reorganizes proprioceptive attention.

- "The knee has an eye on it, and it's seeing a place that it's aiming towards" (FM teacher) — gives the knee perceptual agency
- "The foot just laughs at you" (Feldenkrais) — personification that creates a relationship between self and body part
- "The arm is innocent" (Gibbons) — personification that reframes the student's effort allocation
- When a body part "sees" or "intends," the student's motor planning may shift from central command to distributed organization

**Confidence**: Observed pattern — found across historical and contemporary teachers but not universal. Mechanism plausible from embodied cognition theory but not independently confirmed.

### 12. Imagination as Movement
**Claim**: Language that substitutes imagined movement for physical movement produces nervous system effects equivalent to (or complementary to) actual movement.

- "Now, without doing it, imagine turning your head to the left" → then actually turning, the student finds increased range (Strauss-Klein: taught entire left side through imagination alone)
- "Organize it in your imagination. Get the order sensed and then go ahead" (FM teacher) — motor imagery as explicit preparation step
- "Imagine the intention to lift" (Haller) — the negation frame that activates planning without execution
- Creates motor planning without execution; especially powerful for non-habitual movements where existing motor programs interfere

**Confidence**: High — supported by motor imagery research, found across multiple teachers. The Feldenkrais tradition has used this consistently since its founding.

## II. Compositional Patterns: How Mechanisms Combine

### A. The Awareness Sandwich
A nearly universal pattern in ATM lessons:

1. **Scan** — sense current state (sensory language, questions)
2. **Explore** — try movements (verb frames, constraints)
3. **Rest** — pause to integrate (silence)
4. **Rescan** — sense what changed (same questions as step 1, different answers)

The scan/rescan pair creates a before-after comparison that makes learning *visible to the student*.

[v2] **Corpus status**: Confirmed in 17/19 lessons. The most consistent structural element across all teachers.

### B. The Constraint Ladder
Progressive [v2] ~~narrowing of attention~~ **variation of constraints** within a lesson:

[v2] Takes multiple forms:
- **Progressive narrowing**: Global → Regional → Local → Differential → Reintegrate (original description)
- **Progressive integration**: Part A alone → Part B alone → A+B combined (Schmid-Blackeby)
- **Progressive complexity**: Same theme, increasing constraint count (FM: lift → swing → reach → grasp → open/close → clock)
- **Orientation tour**: Same movement, different body positions (Gibbons: back → stomach → side → sitting)

[v2] **Corpus status**: Confirmed in most lessons, but the original "progressive narrowing" description was too specific. The broader pattern is: **systematic variation of constraints to reveal different aspects of the same movement theme**.

### C. Effort Reduction Arc
Many lessons follow a trajectory from more effort to less:

1. Move with normal effort
2. Make the movement smaller
3. Make it lighter
4. Imagine the movement without doing it
5. Do the movement again — notice the changed quality

The language shifts from imperative → invitation → conditional → imaginal across this arc.

[v2] **Corpus status**: Partially confirmed. Clear in Haller and Strauss-Klein. Many lessons maintain consistent effort throughout (Clark, Schmid-Blackeby). Not universal — more of a stylistic choice than a required pattern.

### [v2] D. Component-then-Assembly
Teach parts separately, then combine:

1. Teach arm movement alone
2. Teach leg movement alone
3. Combine arm + leg with timing constraint
4. Add head, breath, or other components

Different from the Constraint Ladder because the student learns each component before combining, rather than progressively restricting a whole movement.

[v2] **Corpus status**: Found in Schmid-Blackeby, FM Seg 7 Day 8, and several OpenATM lessons. Appears to be a common structural choice for lessons involving bilateral or multi-limb coordination.

### [v2] E. Attention Expansion Arc
Progressive widening of attentional scope:

1. Sense one finger
2. Sense the whole hand
3. Sense hand + wrist + forearm
4. Sense the whole arm
5. Sense both arms
6. Sense the whole self

The complement of the Constraint Ladder (which narrows). Primarily found in body-scan and sensing lessons.

[v2] **Corpus status**: Found primarily in Clark's "Come to Your Senses." May be more common in sensing-only lessons (no physical movement) than in standard ATM.

## III. Anti-Patterns: What Undermines Somatic Learning

[v2] **Revised framing**: The corpus revealed that anti-patterns are more context-dependent than originally described. The core distinction is between language that **interrupts embodied attention** (always harmful) and language that **reframes the meaning of embodied attention** (can be powerful). The anti-patterns below now distinguish these cases.

### 1. Biomechanical Lecturing During Movement
**When harmful**: Anatomical explanation that *replaces* movement attention — the student shifts from sensing to thinking about anatomy.

[v2] **When effective**: Explanatory language that *deepens* the meaning of movement — the student understands *why* the constraint is imposed and engages differently. Gibbons's pedagogical rationales and Feldenkrais's substitution theory both function this way. The key is whether the explanation sends the student *out of* their body or *further into* it.

### 2. Outcome Description Instead of Process
**When harmful**: "Now you should feel more relaxed" — tells the student what to feel rather than letting them discover it. Creates performance pressure or self-doubt.

[v2] **When effective**: Outcome language framed as a question or possibility — "Our bodies are organized to make this possible — can you find how?" (Haller). The outcome becomes a hypothesis to test rather than a benchmark to meet.

### 3. Over-Cuing Without Silence
**When harmful**: Continuous verbal instruction that prevents the nervous system from self-organizing. The student becomes dependent on external direction.

[v2] **When effective**: Dense verbal guidance that builds a detailed spatial map (Gibbons's rib-palpation sections) or maintains a specific quality of attention through a complex coordination (Haller's simultaneous lifting sequences). The distinction is whether the talking is *filling space* or *guiding attention*.

### 4. Vague Sensory Language
"Be aware of your body" — too global to activate any specific sensing. Compare with "Feel the weight of your head on the floor" — specific, locatable, sensorily rich.

[v2] **Corpus status**: Confirmed as harmful across all lessons. However, some intentionally vague language functions as an "open container" — "this experience of yourself" (FM) — that lets each student fill in their own content. The distinction is between *lazy vagueness* (the teacher hasn't thought about what to direct attention to) and *intentional openness* (the teacher trusts the student to find their own focus).

### 5. Corrective Framing
"Don't tense your shoulders" / "Your pelvis should be neutral" — creates a right/wrong frame that activates judgment rather than curiosity. Compare with "What are your shoulders doing right now?" — same information, exploratory frame.

[v2] **Historical note**: Feldenkrais himself used direct correction publicly ("your heel is motionless completely"). But his corrections demonstrated the gap between intention and action rather than prescribing "correct" form. Contemporary teaching has moved away from this, but the Feldenkrais approach was effective because the correction served the lesson's core principle (confronting substitution), not an external standard of correctness.

## Confidence Legend

- **High confidence**: Supported by both linguistic/motor learning theory AND confirmed across diverse speakers in the 19-lesson corpus.
- **Observed pattern**: Consistent in the corpus but not universal across all speakers. May be style-specific.
- **Hypothesis**: Plausible from theory, limited corpus evidence. May be refined or discarded after further evaluation.
