# Speaking Principles

Word choice patterns of Feldenkrais teachers, organized by speaker. These principles help LLMs generate text that sounds like a specific teacher, or understand what a teacher's word choices reveal about their pedagogical intent.

Note: These are observations about **word choice only** — an LLM cannot observe emphasis, tone, cadence, prosody, or pacing.

---

## Andrew Gibbons

Source: "Coordinating Flexors and Extensors" (Cleaning Up the Classics, Lesson 1)
Document ID: `b5255f3e-1789-4d5b-b43a-1d319abed0b0`

### 1. "Source" as a verb for movement origin

Gibbons uses "source" to mean "find the muscular origin of." This is distinctive — most teachers say "initiate" or "begin." "Source" implies a deliberate search for where the effort lives.

> "you're going to want to source the muscular effort that pulls the ribs and the chest a little bit towards the pelvis... from the right side"
>
> "you're really starting to source things from down in the hip joints"
>
> "Just source the muscular work from the left side of the abdomen."

### 2. "Listen for" as the primary sensory verb

Where most teachers say "feel" or "notice," Gibbons frequently says "listen for" or "listen in to." This frames the student as a receiver rather than a doer — paying attention to signals that are already present rather than creating sensations.

> "listen for the work in the fibers that contract on the right half of the abdomen"
>
> "listen in to the left side of your lower back as you tilt over to the left"
>
> "it's the difference between listening and shouting"

### 3. "Emerge" for things that should not be forced

When a result should happen on its own as a consequence of correct organization, Gibbons says it "emerges." This is a pedagogical signal — if you have to force it, you're not doing it the way he intends.

> "the pressure into the foot on the left side will just emerge out of the specificity that you're using in your torso"
>
> "the right foot is just going to get lighter and lighter"
>
> "See if the use of the limbs, the way it happens, emerges out of what you're beginning to understand."

### 4. "Lengthen" for directional cues (especially the pubic bone)

Gibbons' signature cue is "lengthen the pubic bone down away from the head." He uses this phrase (or variants) at least 15 times in a single lesson. "Lengthen" does not mean stretch — it means increase the distance between two landmarks, which implies a specific muscular action (hip flexor engagement) without naming the action.

> "lengthen your pubic bone as part of the way you begin the action"
>
> "lengthen the pubic bone further away from your head before you lift the head or as you lift the head"
>
> "the lengthening comes out of the lengthening of the pubic bone, the use of the torso in this specific way"

### 5. Negation before instruction

Gibbons consistently says what NOT to do before (or instead of) saying what to do. Often the negation gets more emphasis and repetition than the positive instruction. This reflects a core Feldenkrais principle (inhibition of parasitic action) at the level of language.

> "do not contract your abdomen please do not i will say again do not contract your abdomen, if anything, please lengthen your pubic bone"
>
> "try not to move the pelvis by powering it with your lower back... try not to just arch your lower back forward off the floor"
>
> "Don't hold the breath. Don't yank your chin down into your throat."
>
> "you don't need to insist"

### 6. "Sheath" and "fibers" for muscle imagery

Gibbons never refers to muscles as isolated structures. He calls the obliques a "big sheath of muscle" or "huge sheaf of muscle" and consistently talks about the direction of "fibers." This creates an image of continuous fabric rather than discrete mechanical levers.

> "this big sheath of muscle that wraps around the lower half of the cylinder of your torso"
>
> "the direction that your fingers are pointing is the same direction that the fibers of the muscles that they run"
>
> "you want to map your fingers to match the direction of the fibers because that'll help you"

### 7. "Synergy/synergists" for cooperative muscle action

Gibbons explicitly names the concept of synergy — muscles that work together to produce the same movement. This is technical language he doesn't shy away from.

> "these are what they call synergists. These are muscles that work together to produce the same movement"
>
> "see how much more delicate and specific kind of power and synergy you have through your spine"
>
> "the synergies down through your waist make it possible that the head, it rolls, but your head will just fall backwards"

### 8. Anti-effort language

Gibbons consistently frames effort as the enemy. He uses language that drains vigor and insistence from the movement, leaving only precision.

> "use an almost insufficient effort to discover to make the distinction"
>
> "the size of the movement, the vigor of the movement is completely drained of anything"
>
> "the most nuanced, the most comfortable degree, the most quiet quality"
>
> "you don't have to go to kingdom come with your head"

### 9. Pedagogical transparency

Gibbons breaks the fourth wall of teaching — he explains WHY he's giving a particular instruction, names what Feldenkrais was doing in the lesson design, and frames his own additions as additions.

> "This is Dr. Feldenkrais with his engineering hat on, where now he's created a situation where you can really emphasize and recruit and feel more clearly the external obliques"
>
> "the whole point in going slowly is that the teacher has enough time to show even more words into the description"
>
> "the hope is, is that because the arms are kept straight, it's going to elicit something along these lines. But you know, you know different now."

### 10. Analogies grounded in everyday experience

Gibbons uses analogies that are physical, sensory, and accessible. They're not poetic — they're practical.

> "think of your pelvis like a bowl of soup, and you're kind of spilling the soup down in the direction of the feet"
>
> "pushing the accelerator and the brake at the same time"
>
> "the difference between listening and shouting or listening and trying to say something before it's time"
>
> "If you've never dug a hole for yourself, you can probably leave the class now."

### 11. Affirmation markers

Short affirmations woven throughout: "That's right," "That's nice," "Beautiful," "Good." These are rhythmic — they come after the student has had time to try something. They're never effusive.

### 12. "Taste" for quality of experience

Gibbons uses "taste" where others might say "feel" or "experience" — it implies something delicate and fleeting, worth savoring.

> "Taste what the walking feels like."
>
> "Just taste. See what's different."
>
> "your capacity to really taste that quality for yourself and to choose what you want for your life, that's at stake here"

### 13. "Cinching" for lateral shortening

A distinctive word for the side-bending action of the internal obliques — the waist "cinches." This creates a vivid image of gathering fabric.

> "a kind of a cinching of the waist over there a little bit"
>
> "right waistline cinching"
