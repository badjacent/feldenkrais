# Feldenkrais Principles

Reference document for biomechanical and pedagogical principles that are often implicit in Feldenkrais lessons. LLMs have limited training on Feldenkrais method — these principles help identify what a lesson is teaching even when the lesson text never states it directly.

## How to use this document

Each principle includes:
- **What it is**: The principle stated plainly
- **Why it matters for analysis**: What an LLM might miss without knowing this
- **What it looks like in a lesson**: How to recognize the principle at work in movement instructions

---

## 1. Inhibition of parasitic contraction

**What it is**: Many movement problems come not from weakness but from unconscious contractions elsewhere that interfere. A lesson often teaches the student to stop doing something they don't know they're doing.

**Why it matters for analysis**: A step that says "rest" or "pause and notice" isn't filler — it's teaching the nervous system to release contractions that were running in the background. Steps that seem to repeat the same movement may be creating the conditions for a parasitic contraction to release.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Repeated movements with pauses. Instructions to notice effort in unexpected places. Movements that seem too simple to be useful (they're creating contrast so the student can feel what they're holding).

> "inhibit the unconscious contractions in a number of places that get in their way" — Andrew Gibbons, on Coordinating Flexors and Extensors

---

## 2. Synergy between extension and hip flexion

**What it is**: Arching the lower back (lumbar extension), when combined with engagement of the muscles that flex the hip joints, creates a synergy that frees the obliques and other trunk rotators to work without interference.

**Why it matters for analysis**: A step that asks the student to arch the back before a rotation is not just "warming up the spine." It's establishing a specific biomechanical condition — the extension plus hip flexor engagement takes load off the obliques so they can rotate rather than stabilize.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Arching sequences that precede or alternate with rotational movements. Instructions to "let the belly be soft" while arching. Steps that combine crossing legs (hip flexion) with spinal movements.

> "the arching of the lower back, when synergized with the muscles that flex the hip joints, work synergistically to free the obliques to do their work more efficiently and with less interference" — Andrew Gibbons

## 3. The pubic junction as release key

**What it is**: The area around the pubic symphysis — where rectus abdominis, adductors, pelvic floor, and psoas connections converge — acts as a master switch. When this junction is gripped, the whole trunk braces. When it softens, the psoas can work as a hip flexor/connector (rather than a spinal compressor), and the obliques and glutes become available for selective use rather than chronic co-contraction.

**Why it matters for analysis**: This is rarely named in Feldenkrais lessons but is often the hidden target. When a lesson asks you to "let the pelvis be heavy" or "allow the lower belly to soften," it may be aiming at this junction. The LLM should recognize that instructions about pelvic weight, belly softness, or the area between the legs may be targeting this mechanism.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Instructions about letting the pelvis drop or float. Cues about softening the lower belly. Movements that bring attention to the area between the sit bones. Gibbons' language: "pull your pubic bone down between your thighs."

## 4. Proximal conditions enabling distal freedom

**What it is**: Getting the conditions right in one area of the body frees a different area to move efficiently. The area that needs to change is often not the area where the movement happens.

**Why it matters for analysis**: When a lesson spends many steps working on the pelvis before asking for arm or head movements, the pelvic work isn't a separate topic — it's creating the conditions that make the arm/head movement possible without strain. Each step should be interpreted in context of what it enables later.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Long preparatory sequences that seem unrelated to the "main" movement. A pattern of working on the trunk before the extremities. Steps that return to the same foundational position between different movements.

## 5. Clarity of muscular use

**What it is**: Better movement comes not from more effort but from more precise recruitment — using the right muscles "more clearly" while the wrong muscles stay quiet. This is differentiation at the muscular level.

**Why it matters for analysis**: A Feldenkrais lesson almost never asks for more effort. When movements get smaller or slower, the lesson is asking for more precision, not less engagement. The goal is that specific muscles (e.g., left external oblique for rightward rotation) activate while their antagonists and neighbors release.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Instructions to make movements smaller. Cues to use less effort. Asking the student to notice *where* the movement starts. Repeating a movement many times (each repetition is a chance for the nervous system to refine which muscles participate).

> "rotate your spine better, by using the obliques more clearly" — Andrew Gibbons

---

## 6. Differentiation of pelvis and ribcage

**What it is**: The pelvis and ribcage can move independently — rotating in opposite directions, tilting at different rates, or one staying still while the other moves. In habitual movement, they often move as a locked unit. Many lessons teach the student to separate them.

**Why it matters for analysis**: When a lesson asks to turn the head one way and the knees another, or to roll the pelvis while keeping the shoulders still, it is teaching differentiation. The obliques (specifically: left external + right internal for rightward rotation, and vice versa) are the primary steering mechanism for this independence.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Movements where upper and lower body go in different directions. Instructions to hold one part still while moving another. Rolling or tilting the pelvis with attention to what the ribs do (or don't do).

## 7. Ground reaction force as movement resource

**What it is**: The floor is not just a surface to lie on — it's a source of force. Pressing into the floor with one part of the body creates a reaction force that can be transmitted through the skeleton to produce movement elsewhere. Many Feldenkrais lessons are designed around this transmission.

**Why it matters for analysis**: A step that says "press your foot into the floor" isn't about strengthening the leg. It's about creating a force that travels through the skeleton — through the pelvis, up the spine — to produce a rotation or a lift. The analysis should trace the force path, not just describe the local action.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Instructions to press a foot, knee, elbow, or hand into the floor. Movements that begin with a push and end with motion in a distant body part. Lying positions that create specific contact points with the floor.

## 8. Teleceptors and the completion of rotational chains

**What it is**: The eyes (and to a lesser extent, ears) are *teleceptors* — distance sensors that orient the organism in space. In rotation, the eyes are not passive passengers; they are active participants in the movement chain. The vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) naturally counter-rotates the eyes relative to the head to stabilize gaze during movement. This opposition — eyes going one way while the body spirals the other — is a fundamental mechanism of rotation, not a technique specific to any one lesson.

The suboccipital muscles (rectus capitis posterior major/minor, obliquus capitis superior/inferior) form the deepest layer of the neck and connect the atlas and axis to the skull. These muscles are richly innervated with proprioceptors and are directly coupled to eye movement — they respond to where the eyes are looking. When the eyes are fixed at short focal length, the suboccipitals increase their tone, effectively "thickening" the top of the spine and blocking the sequential cascade of vertebral rotation from reaching the skull. When the eyes gaze into the distance, this tone decreases, and the vertebrae at the top of the spine become free to turn relative to the skull.

**Why it matters for analysis**: The eyes are part of the rotational story whether or not the teacher mentions them. A lesson that teaches rotation is necessarily engaging the VOR, the suboccipitals, and the eye-spine coupling — even if the instruction only says "turn your head." When a teacher *does* explicitly invoke the eyes (as a counterweight, as a focal length experiment, as a tracking task), they are making visible a mechanism that is always operative. This means:
- A lesson about rotation that never mentions the eyes is still an eye lesson at the biomechanical level
- A lesson that explicitly uses eye opposition (eyes going opposite to the body's spiral) is revealing how the movement chain completes itself through the teleceptors
- Focal length affects tone: long-distance gaze reduces suboccipital tone, freeing the atlas/axis to participate in rotation; short-distance gaze locks them

**What it looks like in a lesson**: Instructions to let the eyes go in the opposite direction of the movement. Gazing into the distance while rotating. Noticing the quality of rotation with eyes open vs. closed, or near-focus vs. far-focus. Head-pinned-to-floor explorations where the body rotates beneath a stationary skull (the eyes help maintain the head's position). Instructions to "look through" the direction of a movement or to track something visually during a transition.

> "you use the eyes as a counterweight to the knees" — Andrew Gibbons, Carriage of the Head Part 1

> "if you're looking far enough into the distance, the eyes can serve as a very good counterweight to the knees" — Andrew Gibbons, Carriage of the Head Part 1

> "Watch what happens to the quality of the cascade when you keep the focal length short... The breathing may change. The whole tone in the system changes with the focal length." — Andrew Gibbons, Carriage of the Head Part 1

## 9. Attentional occupation as indirect quieting

**What it is**: When a lesson gives the student a demanding, detailed task with one part of the body (e.g., sensing which hand approaches the midline faster), the parts of the body that are not being addressed receive no volitional instruction. This withdrawal of intentional control allows parasitic tone in those uninstructed parts to settle — not through direct inhibition, but through non-occupation. The nervous system stops maintaining habitual holding patterns in parts that aren't being driven.

The lesson then introduces movement in the now-quieted area. Because that area starts from a state of reduced tone, the nervous system gets to experience the movement without its habitual co-contraction pattern. This becomes a new reference point — the system discovers that the movement is possible with far less effort than it usually uses. The real product is a revised motor plan that no longer includes unnecessary recruitment.

**Why it matters for analysis**: A sequence where the student works intensively with the arms and then transitions to leg movements is not just "now we do arms, now we do legs." The arm work is creating conditions in the legs — by leaving them alone. When the analysis identifies a transition from upper body to lower body work (or vice versa), it should consider whether the first section is serving as attentional occupation that quiets the second area.

**What it looks like in a lesson**: A section of detailed, absorbing work with one body region followed by movement in a different region. The second region's movements are often slow and minimal, preserving the quiet state. The teacher may use the same perceptual framework in both sections (e.g., sensing symmetry with the hands, then sensing symmetry with the knees), transferring a skill that was calibrated where proprioception is fine-grained to where it is coarser.

> Observed in **The Plane of Symmetry** (Jeff Haller): The student first brings hands together along an imagined midline plane, sensing asymmetrical rates of approach — a detailed, absorbing task. The legs lie uninstructed and quiet. Then the knees are brought toward and away from the same plane, applying the same perceptual skill to legs that have settled into reduced tone. The sequence transfers both the sensing framework and the quieted state.
