# SPIFFER: A Framework for Understanding the Feldenkrais Method

**Larry Goldfarb, SPIFFER Convention 2014**

*Summary of a two-day presentation on demystifying the Feldenkrais method through seven observable dimensions of movement.*

---

## Overview

SPIFFER is a model developed by Larry Goldfarb to create a common language for understanding and teaching the Feldenkrais method. Drawing on his background in cybernetics, NLP, and his training with Moshe Feldenkrais at Amherst, Goldfarb identifies seven dimensions through which we can observe, understand, and work with human movement.

The model emerged from practical necessity: teaching workplace classes (bus drivers, office workers) required clear, demystified explanations of what the Feldenkrais method actually does.

---

## The Seven Dimensions

### 1. SEQUENCE (S)

**Definition:** The skeletal perspective on movement - how force transmits through bones and joints.

**Three Components:**
- **Transmission** - How force travels through the skeleton (like a ball and chain)
- **Distribution** - How movement spreads across joints ("somatic Marxism: from each according to its ability")
- **Formation** - The shape the body makes during movement

**Key Principles:**
- The skeleton determines what movement is possible
- Joint orientation (facet angle) limits rotation: lumbar spine allows only 5-8 degrees of rotation total; thoracic spine allows much more because facets are oriented differently
- Optimal movement means force goes *through* joints, not *into* them
- The skeleton is designed to support us "for free" - when bones stack properly, muscles don't have to work to hold us up

**Teaching Application:**
- Adding configurational constraints changes *which joints* movement happens in
- Example: Crossing legs and tilting creates a "handle" that transfers rotation into the thoracic spine instead of the hip joint
- The question "movement of ___ in ___" identifies where learning is happening

---

### 2. PATH (P)

**Definition:** The line of movement through three-dimensional space.

**Key Concepts:**
- **Directions:** Forward/back, left/right, up/down, plus inside/out (orientation to internal vs. external world)
- **Dimensions:** Combining two directions creates a dimension
- **Planes:** Combining dimensions creates planes (frontal, sagittal, transverse)
- **Movement types:** Linear (one dimension), arc (one plane), spiral (multiple planes)

**Orientation:**
- Directions are body-oriented, not room-oriented ("up" is always above your head, even when lying down)
- Being awake vs. aware: awareness requires spatial orientation
- "If you don't know where you are, the map doesn't do any good"

**Optimal Path:**
- "The shortest distance between two points might be a straight line, but the easiest path is an arc or a spiral" (attributed to Vince Lombardi)

---

### 3. INITIATION (I)

**Definition:** Where and how movement begins - the "from" of movement.

**Key Insights:**
- Where you start a movement makes a fundamental difference
- Starting the same way every time is a habit (like "riding on top of your movement")
- Alexander Technique focuses on a specific habit of initiation: shortening the back of the neck at the start of any movement

**Optimal Initiation:**
- Generally, starting proximally (from the center/pelvis) is more efficient
- "Move the ball, the rest of you have to follow"
- The pelvis is the biggest bony mass - moving it first recruits larger muscles

**Teaching Application:**
- Changing initiation creates the same mechanical movement with an entirely different neurological pattern
- Example: "Can you start the movement of bringing your legs back by rolling your pelvis?"

---

### 4. FOUNDATION (F)

**Definition:** Your relationship to gravity - base of support and weight distribution.

**Key Concepts:**
- **Base of support** (Moshe's term: "polygon of sustentation")
- Weight can be centered, shifted, or transitioning
- Sitting is "a position around which we can orbit," not a fixed posture

**How We Sense Gravity:**
- We don't sense gravity directly; we sense its consequences
- We infer our orientation from: how bones push on each other, how muscles hang from bones, how we press into the floor
- Postural sway isn't noise - it's the feedback we need to find "up"
- Research shows people balance around the point where they stop accelerating, not around true vertical

**Key Principle:**
- If the skeleton isn't holding you up, muscles have to - and then they can't help you move
- "The division of labor: skeleton holds you up for free; muscles are for movement"

**Design Feature:**
- We're built to be mobile, not stable (small feet, high center of gravity)
- "It just takes a little tilt of the head and you start moving. That's not a problem. That's a feature."

---

### 5. FLOW (F)

**Definition:** Relationship to speed and control.

**Two Aspects:**
1. **Speed/Smoothness** - Smooth (constant speed) vs. saccadic/staccato (stop-start, accelerate-decelerate)
2. **Control** - Bound flow (knowing where you are at every moment) vs. free flow (momentum-driven)

**Moshe's Teaching:**
- "Go quickly without hurrying" - quickly is speed, hurrying is attitude (being ahead of yourself)
- "Go slowly" - slow enough to feel what you're doing
- Reversibility requires bound flow: "at every moment you know where you are"

**Learning State:**
- Most Feldenkrais lessons use bound flow
- "Life isn't reversible. Most movement's about momentum. But when you're learning, you want to get rid of that."

---

### 6. EFFORT (E)

**Definition:** Patterns of muscle action.

**Types of Contraction:**
- **Concentric** - Muscle shortens (picking something up)
- **Isometric** - Muscle contracts without changing length (holding)
- **Eccentric** - Muscle lengthens under control (putting something down slowly)

**Key Insight:**
- Coordination is learned through eccentric contraction
- "Muscles on one side of the joint contract, the other ones have to let go"
- Going slowly forces eccentric control, revealing where muscles "don't know how to lengthen"

**Patterns:**
- **Reciprocal innervation** - Opposing muscles alternate
- **Co-contraction** - Both sides contract simultaneously ("another word for co-contraction is hell")
- Reflexes are built-in patterns that reduce variety (like a marching band knowing formations)

**Optimal Effort:**
- Big muscles make big movements; small muscles guide
- "Coordinated action" (from Higher Judo): proximal muscles initiate, distal muscles refine

---

### 7. RESPIRATION (R)

**Definition:** Breathing patterns and their relationship to movement, consciousness, and emotion.

**Key Principles:**
- Respiration includes all other dimensions (has path, sequence, flow, effort, etc.)
- Directly related to consciousness and emotional state
- "It's a sign of poorly organized movement if you can't breathe and move at the same time"

**Teaching Application:**
- Holding breath keeps ribs from moving, which keeps spine from moving
- Working with respiration frees up movement patterns
- Interfering with breathing patterns can reveal and release habitual holding

## Core Teaching Principles

### Constraints as Teachers

Three types of constraints:
1. **Positional** - Relationship to support surface (foundation)
2. **Configurational** - Relationship to yourself (crossing legs, fixing joints)
3. **Instructional** - Directions given by teacher

"The constraints are your teachers, not me."

By adding constraints:
- You change which joints movement happens in
- You light up different areas kinesthetically (where there's no movement, there's no sensation)
- You create "somatic dissonance" - the experience doesn't match the self-image

### The Indirect Approach

"The way to freedom is through limitation."

- We don't tell people what to learn; we create situations where they discover
- We support the action of chronically contracted muscles, making their work unnecessary
- The message to the brain: "You can hold on as tight as you want, but you can still move"

### Breaking Habits

- Habits are like "a bad smell in the refrigerator - they get into everything"
- You see habits at the very beginning of movement (initiation)
- Breaking habits requires creating "somatic dissonance" - can't say yes and no at the same time

### Perception vs. Structure

"We move according to our self-image, not according to our structure."

- Our kinesthetic sense becomes unreliable through habituation ("sensory motor amnesia")
- We bend our kinesthesia to fit our image
- The work is about helping people perceive themselves differently, not just move differently

## ATM and FI: Same Composition, Different Means

Awareness Through Movement (ATM) and Functional Integration (FI) are "compositionally the same thing."

Both involve:
- Adding constraints to change dynamics
- Highlighting relationships in the kinematic chain
- Working with the same seven dimensions

The difference is pedagogical means:
- ATM: Student creates constraints through self-arrangement
- FI: Practitioner creates constraints through touch and positioning

---

## Philosophical Foundation

**Phenomenology:** Interested in people's experience - that's what we're affecting

**Constructivism:** "Nothing goes in or out of the nervous system. Perception only changes in the ongoing activity of the nervous system."

**From Gurdjieff:** Our nature is to fall asleep (habits become automatic); our job is to keep waking up

**From Kano/Judo:** Developing the potential of bringing body and spirit together

**The Ethical Imperative (from Heinz von Foerster):** "Act to increase the number of choices"

---

## Practical Recommendations

1. **Know where you start** - Everyone is naturally good at one dimension; that's your intuition/habit. Build bridges from there.

2. **Focus narrowly** - Take one aspect of one category and observe only that for a few weeks

3. **Observe everywhere** - "Bowling alleys: eat pizza, drink beer, watch people do the same movements over and over"

4. **The lesson is for later** - "Learning isn't what happens in class. In class, you learn that you can learn. Learning is being able to reproduce that change later."
