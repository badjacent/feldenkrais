The Council of Five

Most of the time, we think of our "self" as a single pilot sitting behind our eyes, pulling levers to move a mechanical body. But when you lie down for a Feldenkrais lesson, that model starts to fall apart. You realize that "you" aren't always the one in charge. Sometimes your neck won't let go, sometimes your ribs feel like a solid block, and sometimes — for a few fleeting moments — movement feels so effortless it's as if the body is moving itself.

The Council of Five is a way of making sense of this. Instead of treating the body as a collection of muscles and bones, we treat it as a group of five distinct "people" living inside us. Each has its own job, its own personality, and its own viewpoint on what we are doing.

Why Characters, Not Concepts?

There's a real quandary in writing about Feldenkrais lessons: any conceptual lens risks turning an experience into an explanation. The lesson happened in your body, not in your head. So why use a lens at all?

Because a character gives you something a concept can't: a narrative. When the Upright Self tells you what they felt when the floor started pushing up through your bones, that's not an analysis — it's a hook. A way to play the lesson back through your body. You read it, and something in your skeleton remembers. The lens isn't there to explain what happened. It's there to help you re-enter what happened.

The Problem: The Shadow

We usually live under the thumb of a sixth character: The Shadow. This is our ego, the one who wants to do the lesson "right," who pushes through pain, and who treats the body like a tool to be used. The goal of a lesson is to wash away the Shadow's dominance so the Council of Five can actually do their jobs.

The Council Members

When the Shadow steps back, we can finally hear the five specialists who actually know how to run a human being:

The Riser
The ancient navigator. They don't care about looking good — they only care about how the skeleton meets the floor to create height.
The Cartographer
The obsessive map-maker. They live for the tiny details — the exact way a finger joints or a rib slides.
The Aligner
The quiet guardian of the horizon. They manage the eyes and the jaw, keeping the internal climate calm and ready.
The Bridge
The diplomat. They ensure the powerful center of the body supports the delicate reach of the hands.
The Guardian
The protector. They decide when it's safe to open up to the world and when we need to curl in for safety.

From Bracing to Transmission

By thinking of these as "people," we change our relationship with ourselves. We stop "correcting" our posture and start negotiating with our Council. As you move through a lesson, you typically pass through three states:

Bracing
The Shadow is in charge. You're gripping, forcing, doing the movement with voluntary effort. The Council can't get a word in.
Softening
The Shadow is losing its grip. You're listening to the Council's feedback — confused in a good way, exploring, negotiating.
Transmission
The Council is in harmony. Movement becomes a clear, skeletal statement of intent. The Shadow has nothing left to do.

Each booklet in this volume listens through the ears of one Council member. If your shoulder is tight, you don't just stretch it — you ask the Guardian why they feel the need to protect you right now. If your movement is jerky, you ask the Cartographer to help you find the missing pixels in your map. We aren't fixing a machine; we are listening to a Council.
